**Group Four: The Chinese and Western Family (Chapter Six, “The Clan”)**

① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China*, I called it an “expanded family.” The core of these terms is “family”: by adding adjectives such as “small” or “large,” they attempt to indicate formal differences between the Chinese and Western “family,” while assuming that the two are essentially of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small clan.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, in terms of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western “family.” The distinction between what we ordinarily call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but rather a matter of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions for being a “large family,” while a family of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two cases are different.

③ The concept of family has a definite definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married couple. The union of this group is for the begetting and rearing of children. In societies where individuals assume the task of bearing and rearing children, such a group cannot be absent. But the procreative function, for each individual family, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage the business of bearing and rearing children of their own, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary groups. Though in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the beginning be prepared for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring the rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature and remains limited to the group composed of parents and children, then at the time of its formation, and again after the children have grown up, there is a period during which it is merely the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they manage are greatly limited. All undertakings that require the cooperation of a larger number of persons must be carried on by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs that this group can manage are also few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members can, according to need, expand outward along the graded order of kinship. The members who make up what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. But the line along which it expands structurally is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a single line: it includes only the paternal side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are outsiders. On the father’s side, however, expansion may go very far. A Chia of “five generations under one roof” may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed according to this unilineal principle of kinship has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a lineage or clan, because what we call a clan is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. Therefore I propose here the term “small clan.” The small clan and the large clan are the same in structural principle; where they differ is in quantity, in size. This is why I do not wish to use the term “large family,” but instead use “small clan.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the clan includes the family; the smallest clan may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The clan is derived from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the clan is only one ring within the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the clan includes the procreative function, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on affairs; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is just like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopts the differential mode of association, using the moral order of kinship relations to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thus making the basic Chia clan-like in nature. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the clan. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity, and this basic social group can by no means be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous: it cannot split up merely because an individual has grown up, nor can it end because an individual has died. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a clan. A clan is by nature long-term, just like our Chia. To call this kind of social group a “small clan” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary nature of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on affairs, and its size is determined by the size of the affairs it undertakes. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be enough to manage it, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife alone can bear, brothers, uncles, and nephews may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society can vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: a unilineal differential pattern.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife are the main axis. Husband and wife jointly carry on the affairs of procreation; children play a secondary role in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Among them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force that holds the group together. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous group for carrying on affairs, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are made, by the needs of collective undertakings, to exclude ordinary personal feelings. By “ordinary personal feelings,” I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can be separated from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private sentiment. In the Chinese family there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “Three Obediences and Four Virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are stressed. All these are characteristics of a social group organized for the carrying on of affairs.
