**Group Four: The Chinese and Western Family  
Chapter Six: “The Lineage”**

① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group ordinarily called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” The principal term in these expressions is “family”; by adding an adjective such as “small” or “large,” one tries to indicate the formal difference between the Chinese and Western “family,” while assuming that they are essentially the same in nature. But I now feel that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage-family.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is to explain, from the standpoint of structural principle, the difference between the Chinese and Western *chia*. What we commonly call the difference between a large family and a small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but a matter of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby satisfy the conditions of a “large family”; a *chia* consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” In numbers, the former is larger than the latter; but structurally the latter is more complex than the former. The principles employed in the two are different.

③ In anthropology the concept of the family has a clear definition: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. This group is formed for the begetting and rearing of children. In societies where the task of producing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, so far as each individual family is concerned, is short-term. When the children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage the procreation of their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary groups; though in fact they are not eternal, they are not provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the function of the family is procreation, it must from the beginning prepare for its own ending. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring the rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The Institution of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when their children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature and remains limited to the group made up of parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is simply a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do indeed engage in economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Any affair requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs that this group can manage are few; its chief function is the procreation and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society the *chia* has no strict corporate boundary. Its members can, according to need, expand outward along the graded order of kinship. The members who constitute what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line of expansion is restricted. The Chinese *chia* expands unilineally: it includes only the father’s side. With few exceptions, a *chia* cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, expansion can go very far. A *chia* with five generations living together may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to this unilineal kinship principle has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our *chia* is a clan. Yet it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a *tsu*, for what we call a *tsu* is composed of many *chia*; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason I propose here the term “small lineage-family.” The small lineage-family and the large lineage are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity or size. This is why I do not wish to use “large family,” but instead use “small lineage-family.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage-family includes the family; the smallest lineage-family may even be identical with the family. For the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage-family is extended out from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage-family is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage-family includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on affairs; if expanded further, it can become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our *chia* is precisely of this sort. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical norms of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various affairs, thereby making the basic *chia* clanlike. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage-family. On the other hand, we may also say that, in order to manage so many affairs, the structure of the *chia* cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group therefore cannot be temporary, as the Western family is. The *chia* must be continuous: it must not split apart because an individual grows up, nor end because an individual dies. Thus the nature of the *chia* becomes that of a lineage. A clan is by nature long-term, just as our *chia* is. To call this sort of group a small lineage-family also indicates this long-term quality, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese *chia* is an organization for carrying on affairs, and the size of the *chia* is determined by the scale of the affairs it undertakes. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the *chia* may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large, exceeding what husband and wife alone can bear, then brothers and paternal uncles may all be gathered into one large *chia*. This explains why the size of the *chia* in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, differential pattern.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly manage the affairs of procreation; children play secondary roles in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Among Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups; they do not fall within the family’s sphere. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes is the cohesive force. The development of sexual and emotional feeling makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the *chia* differs markedly in this respect. Since our *chia* is a continuous group for carrying on affairs, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are nevertheless made, by the needs of the enterprise, to exclude ordinary feelings. By “ordinary feelings” I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No enterprise can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standard of the “Three Obediences and Four Virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are stressed. These are all characteristics of an enterprise-oriented social group.
