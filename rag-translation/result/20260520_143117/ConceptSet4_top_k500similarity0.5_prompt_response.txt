**Group Four: The Chinese and Western Chia**  
**Chapter VI, “Lineage”**

① What I should like to discuss here is the basic social group in our rural society, a group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “expanded family.” In these terms, the principal word is “family,” with an adjective such as “small” or “large” added to indicate a formal difference between Chinese and Western “families,” which are assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia from the standpoint of structural principle. The difference between what we ordinarily call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but a matter of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large family,” while a household consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two are different.

③ The concept of family has a clear definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married couple. This group is united for the begetting and rearing of children. In any society in which the task of bearing and rearing children is assumed by individuals, such a group will not be lacking. But the procreative function, as far as each individual family is concerned, is short-term. When children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to undertake the task of begetting and rearing their own children, generation after generation. The family as a group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite like ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary groups; although in fact they are not eternal, neither are they provisional, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must be prepared from the beginning for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The Institution of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture, the family is always endowed with functions other than reproduction. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to the group composed of parents and children, then both at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up there is a period in which it is merely a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do of course carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can undertake are greatly limited. All matters that require the cooperation of a larger number of persons must be undertaken by other groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate group. As I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs that this group can undertake are few, chiefly the begetting and rearing of children. In Chinese rural society, however, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. The members of this group may, according to need, extend outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who compose what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. But the line along which it expands structurally is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line: it includes only the paternal side. Except in a few special cases, the Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, a son-in-law and a married daughter are both outsiders. On the father’s side, however, expansion can go very far. A Chia in which five generations live under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not quite the same as what we ordinarily call a tsu, for what we call a tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason I propose here the term “small lineage.” A small lineage and a large lineage are the same in structural principle; they differ only in number and size. This is why I do not wish to use “large family,” but instead use “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may be equal to a family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in a social circle. We cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can we say that it forms an independent unit of its own. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of reproduction, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for undertaking affairs; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this sort. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopts the differential mode of association, using the ethical order of kinship to organize social groups and to conduct various undertakings, thus causing the basic Chia to acquire a clan-like character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage; on the other hand, we may also say that, in order to carry out so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children, but must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous: it does not split apart because an individual grows up, nor does it come to an end because an individual dies. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a lineage. A clan is by nature long-term, just as our Chia is. To call this kind of group a “small lineage” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for undertaking affairs, and its size is determined by the size of those affairs. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be enough to deal with it, and the Chia may be so small as to be equal to a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, then brothers, paternal uncles, and nephews may all gather together in one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is the unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife are the main axis. Husband and wife jointly undertake the work of reproduction; the children are supporting characters in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Among Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the family’s sphere. Since husband and wife form the main axis, affection between the sexes becomes the cohesive force. The development of sexual and emotional feeling makes the family the center from which one obtains comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I once used the phrase “a fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia is markedly different in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for undertaking affairs, its main axes are between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. They are vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are nevertheless, under the demands of common undertakings, made to exclude ordinary sentiment. By “ordinary sentiment” I mean that which is opposed to discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese Chia there is family discipline; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standard of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. All these are characteristics of a group organized for the conduct of common undertakings.
