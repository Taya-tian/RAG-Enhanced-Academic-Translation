First Group: Chinese and Western Social Relations  
Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”

① Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a shoulder-load. Every stalk in the load belongs to a definite bundle, sheaf, and handful. Each stalk can also identify those in the same handful, the same sheaf, and the same bundle; the divisions are clear and cannot be confused. In society, these units are groups. When I say that Western social organization is like bundled firewood, I mean that it is often made up of groups formed by a number of people. A group has definite boundaries: who is inside the group and who is outside cannot be vague, but must be clearly distinguished. Those within the group form one body, and their relation to the group is the same. If there are subdivisions or ranks within the same group, these too are stipulated in advance. My analogy of bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may belong to several groups, whereas one stalk of firewood cannot, of course, be found in several sheaves. This is where people differ from firewood. I use the metaphor only to make more concrete a pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call this the group pattern.

② In the West, the family is a clearly bounded group. If a friend writes to say that he will “bring his family” to visit you, he knows very well which people will be coming with him. In China, such a phrase is extremely vague. In England and America, the family includes the man himself, his wife, and their minor children. If he were coming only with his wife, he would not say “family.” In China, although the expression “the whole household will honor us with its presence” is often heard, very few people can say exactly whom this “household” ought to include.

③ Since we have mentioned our terminology, the word chia—“family” or “home”—is perhaps the most elastic of all. “The one at home” may mean only one’s wife; “members of the family line” may refer to a large number of uncles, nephews, and cousins; “one of our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle in order to show intimacy. The range of “our own people” expands and contracts according to time and place. It may become so large as to be beyond counting; truly, all under Heaven may become one family.

④ Why should the term for our most basic social unit be so unclear? In my view, this indicates that our social structure itself differs from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood tied up clearly one by one; rather, it is like the ripples produced when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water, spreading outward circle after circle. Each person is the center of the circles pushed out by his social influence. Those reached by the ripples of these circles become related to him. The circle that a person makes use of at a given time and place is not necessarily always the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society have precisely this character of concentric ripples formed by throwing a stone into water. Kinship relations are social relations arising from the facts of birth and marriage. The network formed through birth and marriage can be extended outward without limit to include an infinite number of people—past, present, and future. The common saying “one maternal cousin extends three thousand li” expresses just this idea; indeed, “three thousand li” merely suggests great breadth. This network is like a spider’s web, with a center, namely oneself. Each of us has such a web spread out through kinship relations, but no two webs cover exactly the same people. People in a society may use the same system to identify their kin; what is the same is only the system. The system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts concerning relationship. But when we use this system to identify concrete relatives and kin, what each person identifies is different. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. Going one step further, there are no two people under Heaven whose recognized kin can be completely identical. Two brothers may indeed have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Therefore, the network of social relations linked together by kinship is particular to the individual. Each network has an “I” as its center, and the center of each network is different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of territorial relations. The modern pao-chia system has the character of the group pattern, but this is entirely incompatible with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each household takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around itself; this circle is the “neighborhood.” When there is a happy occasion, neighbors are invited to drink wine; when a child is born, red eggs are sent; when there is a funeral, they come out to help dress the corpse and carry the coffin. It is an institution of mutual aid in daily life. Yet it is not a fixed group, but a range. The size of this range depends on the strength and influence of the center. The neighborhood of a powerful household may extend over the whole village; that of a poor family may include only the two or three adjacent households. This is the same as our kinship circles. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, for instance, there could live the paternal-aunt’s-side cousin Lin Daiyu and the maternal-aunt’s-side cousin Xue Baochai; later there were even more, such as Baoqin and Xiuyan—anyone who could be connected as kin could be included. But when power changes, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrinks into a small cluster. In the extreme case, it may be like Su Qin returning home in poverty: “his wife did not regard him as a husband, and his sister-in-law did not regard him as an uncle.” The differential mode of association in China’s traditional structure possesses this capacity for expansion and contraction. In the countryside, the family may be very small; but among wealthy landlords and bureaucrats, it may become as large as a small state. Chinese people are especially sensitive to the warmth and coldness of human relations precisely because these highly elastic social circles expand and contract with changes in the power of their center.

⑦ In Western society, where grown children living at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, people recognize the boundaries of the group. Those within the group possess definite qualifications; once those qualifications are lost, they must leave the group. For them, this is not a matter of the warmth or coldness of human feeling, but a matter of rights. In Western society, people struggle over rights; among us, people seek connections and speak of personal ties.
