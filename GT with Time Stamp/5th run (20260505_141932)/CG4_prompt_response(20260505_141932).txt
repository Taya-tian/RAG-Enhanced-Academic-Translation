**Group Four: Chinese and Western “Families” (Chapter Six, “The Family/Lineage”)**

① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, a group commonly referred to as the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China*, I called it the “expanded family.” The central term in these expressions is “family,” with adjectives such as “small” or “large” added to indicate formal differences between Chinese and Western families that are assumed to be essentially the same in nature. Yet I now feel that this terminology is inappropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group of Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the distinction between the Chinese and Western “family” in terms of structural principles. The difference between what we ordinarily call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but rather a matter of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby qualify as a “large family,” whereas a household consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles at work in the two cases are different.

③ In anthropology, the concept of the family has a clear definition: it is a reproductive group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “reproduction” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. The group is formed for the begetting and rearing of children. In any society where the task of childbearing and childrearing is borne by individuals, such a group will necessarily exist. Yet the reproductive function, considered in terms of each individual family, is short-term. Once the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage the business of producing and rearing their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family differs from ordinary social groups. Schools and states are not temporary groups. Although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because their functions are long-term. Since the function of the family is reproduction, it must from the beginning prepare for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring that rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ In every culture, however, the family is always endowed with functions other than reproduction. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end simply because the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature and remains a group limited to parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and after the children have matured there is a period during which it consists only of the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do indeed engage in economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Any undertaking that requires the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have explained above, it possesses strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are quite few, the principal one being the begetting and rearing of children. In Chinese rural society, however, the *jia* has no strict corporate boundary. The members of this group may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship relations. Those who constitute what I have called this “social circle” are not limited to parents and children. Yet the structural path of expansion is restricted. The Chinese *jia* expands along a unilineal line—that is, it includes only the paternal side. With few exceptions, a *jia* cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters alike belong to another family. On the father’s side, however, expansion can extend very far. A household of “five generations under one roof” may include all paternal kin within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our *jia* is a clan. Yet it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a *zu*, for what we call a *zu* is composed of many *jia*; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I introduce here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are identical in their structural principle; their difference lies in quantity, in size. This is why I am unwilling to use the term “large family” and instead use “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may be identical with the family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended outward from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit in itself; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of reproduction, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying out collective undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our *jia* is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopts the differential mode of association, using kinship ethics and relationships to organize social groups and to conduct various undertakings. This causes the basic *jia* to become clan-like in character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to conduct so many undertakings, the structure of the *jia* cannot be limited to the small unit of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, and religious affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group cannot, like the Western family, be temporary. The *jia* must be continuous: it must not divide simply because individuals grow up, nor come to an end because individuals die. Thus the nature of the *jia* becomes that of a lineage. The clan is by nature long-lasting, just as our *jia* is. To call this kind of social group a small lineage is also to indicate this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese *jia* is an organization for the conduct of undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the *jia* may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, then brothers, uncles, and other paternal kin may all be gathered into one large *jia*. This explains why the size of the *jia* in our rural society may vary greatly. Yet no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a reproductive group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relationships among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the central axis. Husband and wife jointly manage the affairs of reproduction; children play a supporting role within the group, and when they grow up they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife become the central axis, and the emotional bond between the sexes serves as the force of cohesion. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes the family a center from which people obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑪ In our rural society, the nature of the *jia* differs markedly in this respect. Since our *jia* is a continuous organization for carrying out undertakings, its central axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the central axis, is not temporary, both axes are shaped by the requirements of collective undertakings and thereby exclude ordinary personal sentiment. By “ordinary personal sentiment,” I mean something opposed to discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To pursue efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be respectful propriety; women are subject to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of an organization devoted to collective undertakings.
