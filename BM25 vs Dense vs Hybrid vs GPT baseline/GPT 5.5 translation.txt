① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, a group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China*, I referred to it as the “expanded family.” The principal term in both of these expressions is “family,” with a qualifying adjective such as “small” or “large” added to indicate formal differences between what are presumed to be, in China and the West, essentially the same kind of “family.” But it now seems to me that this is not appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the standpoint of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western “home” or “family.” The distinction ordinarily drawn between the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size—that is, of the number of persons included in the group—but a matter of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large family,” while a household consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles governing the two are different.

③ In anthropology, the concept of the family has a definite definition: it is a procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the marital pair. This group is formed for the birth and nurture of children. In societies where individuals bear responsibility for procreation and child-rearing, such a group is indispensable. Yet the procreative function, considered in relation to each individual family, is short-term. When children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to conduct their own affairs of having and raising children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not entirely like ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary groups. Although in fact they are not eternal, neither are they provisional, because the functions they perform are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the outset be prepared for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children lies precisely in bringing that rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture, the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not come to an end simply because the children have grown up. If the family does not change its character, and remains a group constituted only by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up there is a period in which it is merely the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife certainly engage in economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can undertake are greatly limited. Matters requiring the cooperation of larger numbers of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are few, the chief one being the procreation and rearing of children. In Chinese rural society, however, the *jia* has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship relations. The members who constitute what I have called this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line along which expansion occurs is restricted. The Chinese *jia* expands along a unilineal route: it includes only the paternal line. With few exceptions, a *jia* cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are members of other families. On the father’s side, however, expansion can extend very far. A household of “five generations under one roof” may include all paternal kin within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed according to this principle of unilineal kinship has a technical name in anthropology: the clan. Structurally, our *jia* is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a *zu*, or lineage, because what we call a *zu* is composed of many *jia*; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are the same in their structural principle; the difference lies in quantity, in size. This is why I do not wish to use the term “large family,” but rather “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be identical with the family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended out from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring within a social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for undertaking affairs; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our *jia* is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the moral relations of kinship to organize social groups and undertake various enterprises, thereby transforming the basic *jia* into something clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to conduct so many affairs, the structure of the *jia* cannot be limited to the small grouping of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, and religious affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group therefore cannot be temporary in the way that the Western family is. The *jia* must be continuous: it must not divide merely because individuals grow up, nor come to an end because individuals die. Thus the nature of the *jia* becomes that of the lineage. The clan is by nature long-lasting, just like our *jia*. To call this kind of social group a “small lineage” also indicates this long-term quality, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese *jia* is an organization for carrying on affairs, and its size is determined by the scale of those affairs. If the affairs are small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the *jia* may be as small as a family. If the affairs are large and exceed what husband and wife can bear, then brothers, paternal uncles, and other agnatic kin may all gather within one large *jia*. This explains why the size of the *jia* in our rural society may vary greatly. Yet regardless of how great the differences in size may be, the structural principle remains consistent: a unilineal, patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to assume many other functions, the content of the relationships among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Together they conduct the affairs of procreation and child-rearing. Children play a secondary role within this group, and once they grow up they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are undertaken by other groups; they do not fall within the family’s sphere. Husband and wife thus become the main axis, and affection between the sexes becomes the force of cohesion. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes the family the center from which comfort in life is obtained. In my book *American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the *jia* differs markedly in this respect. Since our *jia* is a continuous organization for carrying on affairs, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. It is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are nevertheless compelled by the needs of collective affairs to exclude ordinary personal feeling. By “ordinary personal feeling,” I mean feeling as contrasted with discipline. No enterprise can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private sentiment. In the Chinese household there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “Three Obediences and Four Virtues”; and between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are stressed. These are all characteristic features of an enterprise-oriented social group.
