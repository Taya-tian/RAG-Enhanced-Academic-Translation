**Group Two: Chinese and Western Egoism and Individualism (Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”) (1,420 Chinese characters)**

① Centered on the “self,” like a stone cast into water, the social relations that connect one with others are not like the members of a group all standing on a single plane. Rather, they resemble ripples on the surface of water, spreading outward circle by circle: the farther they extend, the more attenuated they become. Here we encounter a fundamental characteristic of Chinese social structure. What Confucianism has examined most carefully is *renlun*—human relations. What, then, is *lun*? My explanation is that it refers to the graded order of successive ripples that arise among the group of people with whom one enters into social relations as they extend outward from oneself. The *Shiming* also says under the entry for *lun* 沦: “It is *lun* 伦; the patterns of water follow one another and possess order.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once observed that all meanings sharing *lun* 仑 as a common denominator are interconnected, “jointly expressing a sense of pattern, classification, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The ten *lun* discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, intimate and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, affairs of government, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to gradations of difference. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father from son, near from far, intimate from distant. *Lun* is an order marked by graded distinctions. To us today, how can concrete social relations such as ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, and husband and wife be placed on the same level as abstract relative positions such as noble and base, intimate and distant, near and far, superior and inferior? In fact, within our traditional social structure, the most basic concept—the normative framework governing the network formed by interactions among persons—is precisely a differential order; that is, it is *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* states: “Loving one’s kin as kin, honoring the honorable, treating elders as elders, and maintaining distinction between men and women—these are not things that can be altered together with the people.” The meaning is that the framework of this social structure cannot be changed; what changes are only the matters carried out by making use of this framework.

③ What Confucius stressed most was the act of “extension” by which the ripples of water spread outward. He first acknowledged a self, the self from which one extends oneself to others; this self must be overcome through ritual propriety—“overcoming the self” is self-cultivation. Following the concentric circles of human relations, one may then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “Those who are filial and fraternal, yet fond of offending their superiors, are rare; those who are not fond of offending their superiors, yet fond of creating disorder, have never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the five human relations as “the universal Way under Heaven.” Because, in this kind of social structure, the movement from the self to all under Heaven proceeds outward circle by circle, Mencius said that he was simply “good at extending.”

④ Within this highly elastic network, there is at every time and in every place a “self” serving as the center. This is not individualism but egoism. The individual is spoken of in relation to the group; it is the member in relation to the collective. Under individualism, on the one hand, there is the idea of equality, meaning that the positions of all members within the same group are equal and that no individual may infringe upon the rights of all; on the other hand, there is the idea of constitutionalism, meaning that the group may not obliterate the individual and may control the individual only with respect to that portion of rights which the individual is willing to surrender. These ideas must first presuppose the existence of the group. In traditional Chinese thought, we do not have this set of ideas, because what we have is egoism: a doctrine in which all values are centered on the “self.”

⑤ Egoism is not limited to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair even if it benefited all under Heaven; Confucianism, too, should be included within it. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu neglected the relativity and elasticity of egoism. He was too rigidly single-minded, clinging stubbornly to the self and refusing to let go. Confucius, by contrast, knew how to extend from the self to others; yet even when this extension reached to the four seas, the center still remained the self. The Master said: “Governing by virtue is like the Pole Star: it dwells in its place, and the multitude of stars pay court to it.” This is an excellent metaphor for the differential mode of association: the self is always the center, like the unmoving Pole Star, while all others revolve around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus possessed a community transcending the individual—he had his Kingdom of Heaven—and therefore he could sacrifice himself to fulfill that Kingdom. Confucius was otherwise.

⑥ Zigong said: “If there were one who broadly bestowed benefits upon the people and was able to aid the multitude, what would you think of him? Could he be called humane?” The Master said: “Why stop at humanity? Such a person would surely be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would still have found this difficult. As for the humane person: wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to attain success himself, he helps others attain success. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy—this may be called the method of humanity.”

⑦ In Confucius’s moral system, he never agreed to depart from the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks it in himself; the petty man seeks it in others.” For this reason, he could not, like Jesus, love all the world universally, even to the point of loving his enemies and asking God to forgive those who killed him—such acts do not proceed from a self-centered starting point. What of Confucius? Someone asked: “What do you think of repaying resentment with virtue?” The Master said: “With what, then, would one repay virtue? Repay resentment with uprightness, and repay virtue with virtue.” This is a hierarchy of differential gradations, and Confucius never relaxed it. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, use the small self to deal with every situation; rather, he expanded or contracted the moral sphere according to need. He was unlike Jesus or China’s Mo Di, for whom, once extension was released, it could not be drawn back.
