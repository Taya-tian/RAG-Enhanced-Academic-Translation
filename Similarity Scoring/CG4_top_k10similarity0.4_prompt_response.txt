① What I wish to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “expanded family.” The central term in these expressions is “family”; an adjective such as “small” or “large” is added to indicate formal differences between Chinese and Western “families” which are assumed to be essentially of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage-family.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the standpoint of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western forms of the chia. The difference between what we commonly call the large family and the small family is certainly not one of size—not a matter of the number of persons included in the group—but one of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions for being a “large family,” while a household consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two are different.

③ In anthropology the concept of the family has a definite definition: it is a procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married pair. This group is formed for the birth and nurture of children. In any society in which individuals bear responsibility for procreation and child-rearing, such a group cannot be lacking. But the function of procreation, considered in terms of each individual family, is short-term. When the children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage the procreation and nurture of their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Social groups such as schools and states are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the beginning be prepared for its own conclusion. The purpose of raising children is precisely to bring child-rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ In every culture, however, the family as a social group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature and remains limited to the group constituted by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is simply a union of husband and wife. Between husband and wife there are, to be sure, economic, emotional, and sexual forms of cooperation; but the affairs they conduct are greatly limited. Matters requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be carried on by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can conduct are also few, chiefly the procreation and nurture of children. In Chinese rural society, however, the chia has no strict corporate boundary. The members of this group can, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who constitute what I have called this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet structurally, the line along which it expands is restricted. The Chinese chia expands along a unilineal line; that is, it includes only the patrilineal side. With few exceptions, the chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are members of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, it can expand very far: a chia of five generations under one roof can include all patrilineal kin within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our chia is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we commonly call a tsu, for what we call a tsu is composed of many chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason I introduce here the term “small lineage-family.” The small lineage-family and the large lineage-family are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity, or size. This is why I do not wish to use the term “large family,” but instead use “small lineage-family.” The difference of a single word explains the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage-family includes the family. The smallest lineage-family may even be equal to the family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage-family is extended from the basis of the family. But the family included within the lineage-family is only one ring in a social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate body.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in character. Although the lineage-family includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our chia is precisely like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopts a differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to conduct various undertakings, so that the basic chia becomes clan-like in character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage-family. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary, as the Western family is. The chia must be continuous: it must not split apart when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the character of the chia becomes that of a clan. The clan is by nature long-term, just as our chia is. To call this sort of social group of ours a “small lineage-family” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese chia is an organization for carrying on undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient to manage it, and the chia may be as small as the family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife alone can bear, brothers, elder and younger uncles, and others may all be gathered into one large chia. This explains why the size of the chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: a unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly conduct the affairs of procreation; children are supporting figures in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Among them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes is the force of cohesion. The development of sexual and emotional attachment makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the phrase “a fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the character of the chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our chia is a continuous organization for carrying on undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are nevertheless made, by the needs of the undertaking, to exclude ordinary sentiment. By “ordinary sentiment” I mean sentiment as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of a social group organized for carrying on undertakings.
