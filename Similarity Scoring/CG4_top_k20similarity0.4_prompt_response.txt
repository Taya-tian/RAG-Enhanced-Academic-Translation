① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” In these terms, the substantive word is “family,” with an adjective such as “large” or “small” added to indicate differences in form between the Chinese and Western “family,” presumed to be the same in nature. But it now seems to me that this is not appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the standpoint of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia. The difference between what we ordinarily call a large-family and a small-family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions for being a “large-family,” while a family of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small-family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two cases are different.

③ The concept of family has a clear definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” indicates its structure; “procreation” indicates its function. The parent-child tie is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. This group is united for the begetting and rearing of children. In societies where individuals bear the responsibility for producing and rearing children, such a group cannot be absent. But the procreative function, for each individual family, is short-term. Once the children have grown up, they leave the nurture of their parents and go on to conduct the business of begetting and rearing their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite like ordinary social groups. Schools and states are not temporary groups; although in fact they are not eternal, neither are they ad hoc, because the functions they perform are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must, from the beginning, be prepared for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture, the family as a social group is always endowed with functions beyond procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains confined to the group formed by parents and children, then at the time of its formation and again after the children have grown up there is a period in which it consists only of the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife may indeed carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Any undertaking requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are few, chiefly the begetting and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who constitute what I have called the “social circle” are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which it expands structurally is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands unilineally: it includes only the father’s side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are members of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, expansion can go very far. A Chia of “five generations under one roof” may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. Yet it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a *tsu*, for what we call a *tsu* is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason I introduce here the term “small lineage.” A small lineage and a large lineage are the same in their structural principle; their difference lies in number and size. This is why I do not wish to use “large-family,” but instead “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural character of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family. The smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family, because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relationship, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is derived from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring within a social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the procreative function, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, a clan is an organization for collective undertakings; expanded further, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thus making the basic Chia clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to manage so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be enlarged. Moreover, political, economic, and religious affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary in the way the Western family is. The Chia must be continuous: it must not split apart because an individual grows up, nor come to an end because an individual dies. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a lineage. The clan is by nature long-term, just like our Chia. To call this kind of social group a “small lineage” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for undertakings, and the size of the Chia is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient to handle it, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large, exceeding what husband and wife alone can bear, brothers, elder and younger paternal uncles, and others may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife are the main axis. Husband and wife jointly conduct the business of procreation; children play a secondary role in the group, and once they have grown up they leave it. Among Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes is the force that holds the group together. The development of sexual affection makes their family the center in which comfort in life is obtained. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑪ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes exclude ordinary sentiment because of the needs of the undertaking. By “ordinary sentiment” I mean something contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; for women there are the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all features of an undertaking-oriented social group.
