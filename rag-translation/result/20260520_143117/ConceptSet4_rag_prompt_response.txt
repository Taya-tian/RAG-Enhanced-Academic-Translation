**Group Four: The Family in China and the West—Chapter Six, “Lineage”**

① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “expanded family.” In both of these terms the main word is “family,” with an adjective such as “large” or “expanded” added to indicate a formal difference between Chinese and Western families, which are assumed to be essentially of the same nature. But I now feel that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group of Chinese rural society would be “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, in terms of structural principles, the distinction between the Chinese and Western “family.” The difference between what we ordinarily call the large-family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby qualify as a “large-family,” while a family consisting only of parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—four persons in all—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two are different.

③ The concept of the family has a clear definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married couple. This group is united for the birth and nurture of children. In any society where the task of bearing and rearing children is assumed by individuals, such a group cannot be absent. But the procreative function, so far as each individual family is concerned, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to carry out their own task of bearing and rearing children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Groups such as schools and states are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not ad hoc, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the very beginning be prepared to come to an end. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in any culture, the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not cease when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its character and remains a group constituted solely by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and again after the children have grown up, there is a period during which it is merely a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife may, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Matters requiring the cooperation of a larger number of persons must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundaries. Its members can be extended outward according to need, along the graded order of kinship. The members who constitute what I call the social circle are not limited to parents and children. But structurally the line along which it expands is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands unilaterally; that is, it includes only the father’s side. With few exceptions, the Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law at the same time. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are both people of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, extension may go very far. A Chia in which five generations live together may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to the principle of unilateral kinship has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. Yet it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a Tsu, for what we call a Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small lineage.” A small lineage and a large lineage are the same in their structural principle; their difference lies in quantity, in size. This is why I do not wish to use the term “large-family,” but prefer “small lineage.” A difference of one word reveals the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family. The smallest lineage may be equivalent to a family. For the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in the social circle. We cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can we say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is just like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relationships of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thereby making the basic Chia take on the character of a clan. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, all political, economic, and religious affairs require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous: it must not split up when an individual grows up, nor come to an end when an individual dies. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a lineage. A clan is by nature long-lasting, as is our Chia. To call this kind of social group a small lineage also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary nature of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient to manage it, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, then brothers, uncles, and nephews may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the differences in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is the unilateral, patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly carry on the work of procreation, while the children play a secondary role in the group; when they grow up, they leave it. Among Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife therefore become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force that binds the group together. The development of sexual affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for carrying on undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are shaped by the needs of the undertaking and exclude ordinary sentiment. By “ordinary sentiment” I mean that which stands in contrast to discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there is family discipline; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; and between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of an undertaking-oriented social group.
