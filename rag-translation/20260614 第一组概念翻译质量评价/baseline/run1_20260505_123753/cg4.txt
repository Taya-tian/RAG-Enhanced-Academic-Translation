① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, a group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China*, I referred to it as the “expanded family.” The central term in these expressions is “family,” with adjectives such as “small” or “large” added to indicate formal differences between what are assumed to be essentially the same kind of “family” in China and the West. Yet, as I now see it, this terminology is not quite appropriate. A more accurate designation for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the distinction between the Chinese and Western “family” in terms of structural principles. The difference between what we ordinarily call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but rather of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions for being a “large family,” while a household consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, their son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles on which the two are organized are different.

③ In anthropology, the concept of the family has a clear definition: it is a reproductive group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “reproduction” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. This group is formed for the birth and rearing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a group is indispensable. Yet the reproductive function, with respect to each individual family, is short-term. Once the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to undertake the work of bearing and rearing children of their own, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not entirely like ordinary social groups. Groups such as schools or states are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes reproduction as its function, it must from the outset prepare for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring childrearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture, the family is always endowed with functions other than reproduction. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end simply because the children have grown up. If the family does not change in nature, and remains a group constituted only by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and after the children have matured there is a period in which it consists merely of the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife certainly engage in economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation; nevertheless, the range of affairs they undertake is greatly limited. Any undertaking that requires the cooperation of a larger number of people must be carried out by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are quite few, the principal one being the reproduction and rearing of children. In Chinese rural society, however, the *jia* does not have strict corporate boundaries. Its members may expand outward as needed along the graded order of kinship relations. The members constituting what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the route of expansion is restricted. The Chinese *jia* expands along a unilineal route: it includes only the paternal line. With few exceptions, a *jia* cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, the son-in-law and the married daughter both belong to outside families. On the father’s side, however, the scope of expansion can extend very far. A household of “five generations under one roof” may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to this unilineal principle of kinship has a technical name in anthropology: the clan. Structurally, our *jia* is a clan. Yet it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a *zu*, or lineage, because what we call a *zu* is composed of many *jia*; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are identical in their structural principle; what differs is their quantity or size. This is why I prefer not to use “large family,” but instead “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to the family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation—the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended from the basis of the family. Yet the family included within the lineage is only one ring within the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in character. Although the lineage includes the function of reproduction, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for collective undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our *jia* is precisely of this sort. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopts a differential mode of association, using the ethical order of kinship relations to organize social groups and to carry out various enterprises. In this way, the basic *jia* becomes clan-like in nature. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry out so many undertakings, the structure of the *jia* cannot be limited to the small grouping of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group therefore cannot be temporary, as the Western family is. The *jia* must be continuous: it must not split apart when individuals mature, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the *jia* becomes that of the lineage. The clan is by nature long-term, just as our *jia* is. To call this kind of social group a “small lineage” is also to indicate this long-term quality, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese *jia* is an organization for collective undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient to handle it, and the *jia* may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife alone can bear, then brothers, elder and younger paternal uncles, and other agnatic kin may all be gathered into one large *jia*. This explains why the size of the *jia* in our rural society can vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a reproductive group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relationships among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the central axis. Together they carry out the work of reproduction and childrearing; children play a secondary role within this group, and when they grow up they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife thus become the central axis, and affection between the sexes is the force that binds the group together. The development of sexual and emotional bonds makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the *jia* differs markedly in this respect. Since our *jia* is a continuous social group organized for collective undertakings, its central axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes exclude ordinary sentiment because of the needs of collective undertakings. By “ordinary sentiment,” I mean sentiment as opposed to discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To pursue efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are subject to the standards of the “Three Obediences and Four Virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. All these are distinctive features of a social group organized for collective undertakings.
