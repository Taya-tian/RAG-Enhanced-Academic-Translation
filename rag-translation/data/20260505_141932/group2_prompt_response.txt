**Group Two: Chinese and Western Egocentrism and Individualism  
(Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”)**

① Taking the “self” as the center, like a stone cast into water, the social relationships formed with others are not like the members of an organization all standing on a single plane. Rather, they resemble ripples on the surface of water, spreading outward circle by circle: the farther they extend, the more distant and attenuated they become. Here we encounter a basic characteristic of Chinese social structure. What Confucianism has examined most closely is *renlun*—human relations. What is *lun*? My interpretation is that it refers to the differentiated order of the successive circles of ripples that arise among those people who, extending outward from oneself, enter into social relations with oneself. The *Shiming* also says under the character *lun* 沦: “*Lun* means order; the patterns of water follow one another in sequence and thus possess order.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once said that all meanings sharing *lun* 仑 as a common component are connected: they “jointly express the ideas of pattern, category, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* emphasizes distinction. The “ten *lun*” discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—spirits and humans, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, intimate and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, political affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means properly distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order of gradations. To us today, how can concrete social relationships such as spirits and humans, ruler and minister, father and son, husband and wife be placed in the same category as abstract relative positions such as noble and base, intimate and distant, near and far, superior and inferior? In fact, within our traditional social structure, the most basic concept—the normative framework governing the network formed by interactions among persons—is precisely a differential order; that is, *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: “Loving one’s kin as kin, honoring the honorable, respecting elders as elders, and maintaining distinctions between men and women—these are things that cannot be altered among the people.” This means that the framework of this social structure cannot be changed; what changes are only the particular matters carried out by making use of this framework.

③ What Confucius emphasized most was the word “extend” (*tui*), as in the outward expansion of ripples on water. He first acknowledges a self, a self from which one extends oneself to others; as for this self, it must be restrained through ritual propriety. “Overcoming the self” is self-cultivation. Following the concentric circles of human relations, one may then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “Those who are filial and fraternal yet fond of offending their superiors are few indeed; those who are not fond of offending their superiors yet fond of creating disorder have never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the five relations as “the universal Way of all under Heaven.” Because, in this kind of social structure, one proceeds from the self to all under Heaven by extending outward circle by circle, Mencius said that he was merely “good at extension.”

④ In this highly elastic network, there is always, at every time and place, a “self” serving as the center. This is not individualism, but egocentrism. The individual is spoken of in relation to the group: the individual member in relation to the collective. Under individualism, on the one hand there is the concept of equality, meaning that the positions of all members within the same group are equal and that no individual may infringe upon the rights of the whole; on the other hand there is the concept of constitutionalism, meaning that the group may not obliterate the individual and may control the individual only with respect to that portion of rights which the individual is willing to surrender. Such ideas must first presuppose the existence of the group. In traditional Chinese thought we do not have this set of ideas, because what we have is egocentrism: a doctrine in which all values take the “self” as their center.

⑤ Egocentrism is not confined to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair even if it benefited all under Heaven; Confucianism too should be included within it. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu overlooked the relativity and elasticity of egocentrism. He was too rigid, clinging obstinately to the self and refusing to let go. Confucius, by contrast, knew how to extend from himself to others; yet even when this extension reached to the Four Seas, the center remained the self. Confucius said: “To govern by virtue is like the Pole Star: it remains in its place, while the multitude of stars revolve around it.” This is an excellent metaphor for the differential mode of association: the self is always the center, like the Pole Star that does not shift with the seasons, while all other people revolve around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus possessed a collectivity transcending the individual—he had his Kingdom of Heaven—and therefore he could sacrifice himself in order to fulfill that Kingdom. Confucius was not so.

⑥ Zigong said: “If there were one who broadly conferred benefits upon the people and was able to aid the multitude, what would you say of him? Could he be called humane?” The Master said: “Why stop at humaneness? He would surely be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would have found this difficult. As for the humane person: wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to attain success himself, he enables others to attain success. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy—this may be called the method of humaneness.”

⑦ In Confucius’s moral system, he never departs from the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks it in himself; the petty man seeks it in others.” For this reason, he could not, like Jesus, love all under Heaven universally, even loving his enemies and praying to God to forgive those who killed him—such acts do not proceed from a self-centered point of departure. What about Confucius? Someone asked: “To repay resentment with virtue—what do you think of that?” The Master said: “Then with what would one repay virtue? Repay resentment with uprightness; repay virtue with virtue.” This is the hierarchy of the differential order, and Confucius would never relinquish it. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, use the narrow self to respond to every situation; rather, he expanded or contracted the scope of morality according to need. He was unlike Jesus or China’s Mo Di, for whom, once love was extended outward, it could not be drawn back.
