**Group Four: The Chinese and Western “Chia” — Chapter Six, “Clan-Family”**

① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group ordinarily called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” The subject of these terms is “family”; an adjective such as “large” or “expanded” is added to indicate the formal difference between Chinese and Western “families,” which are assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group of Chinese rural society would be “small clan-family.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is to explain the difference between the Chinese and Western “family” from the standpoint of structural principle. The difference between what we ordinarily call a large-family and a small-family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions of a “large-family,” while a group consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small-family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two are different.

③ In anthropology the concept of the family has a definite definition: it is a procreative group formed by parents and children. “Parents and children” indicate its structure; “procreation and rearing” indicate its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married couple. This group is united for the birth and rearing of children. In any society in which individuals bear the task of producing and rearing children, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, so far as each individual family is concerned, is short-term. When the children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to conduct their own affairs of producing and rearing children, generation after generation. The family group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family group is not quite like ordinary social groups. Groups such as schools and states are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation and rearing as its function, it must be prepared from the beginning for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family group is always endowed with functions other than procreation and rearing. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to the group formed by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is merely a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife, to be sure, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation; but the affairs they can conduct are greatly limited. All matters requiring the cooperation of a larger number of persons must be conducted by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can conduct are few, chiefly the procreation and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. The members of this group may, according to need, extend outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who make up what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which it expands is structurally restricted. The line of expansion of the Chinese Chia is unilineal—that is, it includes only the patrilineal side. Except in a few cases, the Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law at the same time. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of an “outside family.” On the father’s side, however, expansion may go very far. A Chia of five generations living under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not exactly the same as what we ordinarily call a Tsu, for what we call a Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason I propose here the term “small clan-family.” The small clan-family and the large clan are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity and size. This is why I prefer not to use “large-family,” but “small clan-family.” A difference of one word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the clan-family includes the family; the smallest clan-family may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The clan-family is extended outward from the basis of the family. But the family included within the clan-family is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own. It is not a corporation.

⑧ Differences in form also bring changes in nature. Although the clan-family includes the function of procreation and rearing, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on affairs; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical order of kinship to organize social groups and carry on various undertakings, thus making the basic Chia clan-like in character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the clan-family. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary in the manner of the Western family. The Chia must be continuous: it must not divide simply because an individual has grown up, nor end because an individual has died. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of the clan. The clan is by nature long-term, just as our Chia is. To call this social group a “small clan-family” is also to indicate this long-term character, in contrast with the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on affairs, and its size is determined by the scale of those affairs. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient to handle it, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large, exceeding what husband and wife can bear, brothers, paternal uncles, and others may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But however great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is the unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family corporation, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly conduct the affairs of procreation and rearing; children are secondary figures in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Among them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other corporations and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Since husband and wife become the main axis, the affection between the sexes is the cohesive force. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes the family the center from which comfort in life is obtained. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for carrying on affairs, its main axes lie between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; they are vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are, by the needs of the undertaking, made to exclude ordinary sentiment. By “ordinary sentiment” I mean sentiment as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can escape considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the tolerance of private feelings. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; for women there are the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. All these are characteristics of a social group organized for the conduct of affairs.
