**Group Two: Chinese and Western Egoism and Individualism (Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”)**

① Taking the “self” as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like the members of a group all standing on one plane. They are rather like ripples on water, pushed outward circle by circle: the farther they extend, the more distant and the thinner they become. Here we encounter a basic characteristic of Chinese social structure. What Confucianism has examined most carefully is *human relations*—*lun*. What is *lun*? My interpretation is that it is the differential order of the successive circles of ripples that arise among those people who, extending outward from oneself, enter into social relations with oneself. The *Shiming*, under the character *lun* 沦, also says: “*Lun* means order; the patterns of water follow one another and have an orderly sequence.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once said that all meanings sharing *lun* 仑 as a common element are connected: “What they jointly express is a sense of pattern, category, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* stresses distinctions. The ten *lun* spoken of in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—spirits and men, ruler and subject, father and son, noble and humble, close and distant kin, rank and reward, husband and wife, political affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to gradations. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father from son, near from far, close kin from distant kin. *Lun* is an order with graded differences. To us today, how can such concrete social relations as spirits and men, ruler and subject, father and son, husband and wife be placed on the same level as such abstract relative positions as noble and humble, close and distant, near and far, superior and inferior? In fact, in our traditional social structure, the most basic concept—the guiding order in the network formed by intercourse between people—is precisely a differential sequence; that is, *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: “Loving one’s kin as kin, honoring the honorable, treating elders as elders, and distinguishing between men and women—these are not things that can be changed among the people.” The meaning is that the framework of this social structure cannot be changed; what changes are only the things done by making use of this framework.

③ What Confucius emphasized most was the word “extend” in the outward expansion of ripples on water. He first acknowledged a self, a self from which one extends to others; and this self must be restrained by ritual propriety. To overcome the self is to cultivate the person. Following the concentric circles of human relations, one can then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “Those who are filial to parents and respectful to elder brothers, yet fond of offending their superiors, are rare; those who are not fond of offending their superiors yet fond of making disorder—there have never been such people.” From self to family, from family to state, from state to all under heaven, there is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* takes the five relations as “the universal Way under heaven.” Because in this kind of social structure one extends circle by circle from the self to all under heaven, Mencius said that he was merely “good at extending.”

④ In this highly elastic network, there is always, at any time and place, a “self” as the center. This is not individualism, but egoism. The individual is spoken of in relation to the group: the member in relation to the group. Under individualism, on the one hand there is the idea of equality, meaning that the positions of all members within the same group are equal, and that the individual cannot infringe upon the rights of all; on the other hand there is the idea of constitutionalism, meaning that the group cannot obliterate the individual, and can control the individual only within that portion of rights which the individual is willing to surrender. These ideas must first presuppose the existence of the group. In our traditional Chinese thought there is no such set of ideas, because what we have is egoism: a doctrine in which all values take the “self” as their center.

⑤ Egoism is not limited to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair to benefit the whole world; even Confucianism should be included within it. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu ignored the relativity and elasticity of egoism. He was too rigid: once he seized upon the self, he would not let go. Confucius, by contrast, knew how to extend from the self to others. Yet even if this extension reached to the four seas, the center still remained the self. The Master said: “To govern by virtue is like the North Star: it dwells in its place, and the multitude of stars revolve around it.” This is a very good metaphor for the differential mode of association. The self is always the center, like the North Star that does not shift with the seasons; all others revolve around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus had a group transcending the individual; he had his Kingdom of Heaven, and so he could sacrifice himself to fulfill the Kingdom of Heaven. Confucius was not so.

⑥ Zigong said: “If there were someone who bestowed broadly upon the people and was able to help the multitude, what would you say of him? Could he be called humane?” The Master said: “Why stop at humanity? He would surely have to be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would have found this difficult. As for the humane person: wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to attain success himself, he helps others attain success. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy—this may be called the method of humanity.”

⑦ In Confucius’s moral system, he never allowed departure from the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks within himself; the petty man seeks in others.” For this reason, he could not, like Jesus, love all under heaven universally, even loving his enemies and praying to God to forgive those who killed him—such things do not start from the self as center. What of Confucius? Someone asked: “Repaying resentment with virtue—how would that be?” The Master said: “Then with what would one repay virtue? Repay resentment with uprightness; repay virtue with virtue.” This is a differential hierarchy, and Confucius would never let it go. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, use the small self to deal with every situation. He expanded or contracted the scope of morality according to need. He was not like Jesus, or like China’s Mo Di, who, once they extended it, could not draw it back.
