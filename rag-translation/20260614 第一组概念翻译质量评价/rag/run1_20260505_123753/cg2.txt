① Taking the “self” as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like the members of a group all standing on the same plane. Rather, they are like the ripples on the surface of water, spreading outward circle by circle: the farther they spread, the more distant and the thinner they become. Here we encounter the basic characteristic of Chinese social structure. What our Confucian tradition has studied most carefully is *lun*—human relations. What is *lun*? My explanation is that it is the differential order of the successive ripples that arise among the group of people who, spreading outward from oneself, enter into social relations with oneself. The *Shiming*, under the character *lun* 沦, also says: “It means *lun* 伦; the patterns on water follow one another and have order.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once said that all meanings sharing *lun* 仑 as their common element are connected: “What they jointly express is a sense of pattern, category, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. In the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*, the ten *lun*—spirits and men, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, intimate and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, political affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order with gradations. To us today, how can concrete social relations such as spirits and men, ruler and minister, father and son, husband and wife be placed on the same level as abstract relative positions such as noble and base, intimate and distant, near and far, superior and inferior? In fact, in our traditional social structure, the most basic concept—the guiding order in the network formed by interpersonal intercourse—is precisely a differential order, that is, *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: “Loving one’s kin as kin, honoring the honored, respecting elders as elders, and maintaining distinctions between men and women—these are things that cannot be changed together with the people.” This means that the framework of this social structure cannot be changed; what changes is only the use made of this framework.

③ What Confucius emphasized most was the word “extend” in the outward expansion of the ripples. He first acknowledged a self, a self from which one extends oneself to others. This self must be subdued by ritual; to subdue the self is to cultivate the person. Following the concentric circles of human relations, one may then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “A man who is filial to his parents and respectful to his elder brothers will rarely be fond of offending his superiors; one who is not fond of offending his superiors has never been fond of making disorder.” From self to family, from family to state, from state to all under Heaven—this is one continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the five relations as “the universal Way under Heaven.” Because in this kind of social structure the movement from self to all under Heaven proceeds circle by circle outward, Mencius said that he was simply “good at extending.”

④ In this highly elastic network, there is always, at every time and place, a “self” as the center. This is not individualism, but egoism. The individual is spoken of in relation to the group; it is the member in relation to the group. Under individualism, on the one hand there is the idea of equality, meaning that the positions of all members within the same group are equal and that no individual may infringe upon the rights of all the others; on the other hand there is the idea of constitutionalism, meaning that the group cannot obliterate the individual, but may control the individual only within that portion of rights which the individual is willing to surrender. These ideas must first presuppose the existence of a group. In our traditional Chinese thought there is no such set of ideas, because what we have is egoism: a doctrine in which all values take the “self” as their center.

⑤ Egoism is not limited to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair even if it benefited all under Heaven; even the Confucians should be included. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu ignored the relativity and elasticity of egoism. He was too rigid, clinging stubbornly to the self and refusing to let go. Confucius, by contrast, knew how to extend from himself to others; yet even when this extension reached to the four seas, the center remained the self. The Master said: “Governing by virtue may be compared to the North Star: it stays in its place, and all the other stars revolve around it.” This is a very good metaphor for the differential mode of association: the self is always the center, like the Pole Star, unmoving through the seasons, while all other people revolve around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus had a group that transcended the individual; he had his Kingdom of Heaven, and so he could sacrifice himself in order to fulfill the Kingdom of Heaven. Confucius was not so.

⑥ Zigong said: “If there were one who broadly bestowed benefits upon the people and was able to aid the multitude, what would you say of him? Could he be called humane?” The Master said: “Why stop at humanity? He would surely have to be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would still have found this difficult. As for the humane man: wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to make himself succeed, he helps others succeed. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy—this may be called the method of humanity.”

⑦ In Confucius’s moral system, he was absolutely unwilling to leave the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks it in himself; the petty man seeks it in others.” Therefore, he could not, like Jesus, love all under Heaven universally, even loving his enemies and praying to God to forgive those who killed him—these things do not start from the self as center. What about Confucius? Someone asked: “What do you think of repaying injury with virtue?” The Master said: “Then with what would you repay virtue? Repay injury with uprightness, and repay virtue with virtue.” This is a hierarchy of differential levels, and Confucius would never relax it. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, deal with every situation from the standpoint of a narrow self; he expanded or contracted the moral sphere according to need. He was not like Jesus, or China’s Mo Di, who, once they let it expand, could not draw it back.
