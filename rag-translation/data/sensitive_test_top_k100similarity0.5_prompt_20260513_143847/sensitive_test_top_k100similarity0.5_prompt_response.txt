1. Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle straw in the fields: several stalks are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a bundle, several bundles into a larger bundle, and several large bundles into a carrying load.

2. In China, this saying is extremely ambiguous.

3. This network is like a spider’s web: it has a center, and that center is oneself.

4. The concept of the “citizen” must first presuppose the existence of a “state.”

5. A group of people whose lives depend on one another cannot survive alone and scattered in the mountains and forests.

6. The constitutional concept is coordinated with the Western concept of public affairs.

7. This social group is joined together for the bearing and rearing of children.

8. On the father’s side, however, it can be extended very far. A five-generation household may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes is the force of cohesion.

10. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of living” to describe it.

11. “Those at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “the family gate” may refer to a large group of uncles, cousins, nephews, and others; “one of our own people” may include anyone whom one wants to draw into one’s circle and toward whom one wishes to show intimacy. The range of “our own people” expands and contracts according to time and place. It can become so large as to be uncountable—indeed, the whole world may be made into one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I have called the “differential mode of association”: a network formed by one private tie after another. This pattern is different from the modern Western “organizational mode of association.”

13. But it now seems to me that this formulation is not quite satisfactory. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be “small lineage.”

14. Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family. For the foundation of the kinship structure is the parent-child relationship—the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, a clan is a corporate organization; when enlarged further, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, used the ethical norms of kinship to organize social groups and carry on various enterprises, and thus made the basic family take on the character of a clan.

17. The Chinese family is a corporate organization; its size is determined by the size of the enterprise it undertakes.

18. Since our family is a continuous corporate community, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. It is vertical, not horizontal.

19. In using this analogy, I wish to make more concrete a certain pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system belongs to the organizational mode of association, but it is quite incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward without limit, including people of the past, the present, and the future. Our common saying, “one cousinship extends three thousand li,” expresses just this idea. In fact, “three thousand li” merely indicates the great breadth of its reach.

22. In the kinship system, each of us has parents; but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people in the world whose acknowledged kin can be completely identical. Two brothers, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is much like our circles of kinship. In the Grand View Garden of the Jia family, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin on the paternal-aunt’s side, and Xue Baochai, a cousin on the maternal-aunt’s side; later there were still more—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and so forth. Anyone who could be connected as a relative could be included. But once power and influence changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrank into a small cluster.

24. Taking the self as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like members of an organization all standing on the same plane. They are like ripples on the surface of the water, pushing outward circle after circle: the farther they extend, the more distant and the thinner they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The ten *lun* discussed in the “Ji Tong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—between spirits and men, ruler and subject, father and son, noble and base, close and distant kin, rank and reward, husband and wife, government affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded distinctions. “Not losing the proper *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order of graded differences.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “Those who are filial and fraternal, yet fond of offending their superiors, are few indeed; those who are not fond of offending their superiors, yet fond of making rebellion, have never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to the world—this is a continuous path. In the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the five relationships are called “the universal Way of the world.”

27. A group is a “reality” transcending the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot point to a concrete object with physical form and say, “This is the group.” It is a bundle of relationships among people, a force that controls the conduct of each individual, an object upon which the lives of its constituent members depend; it is a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot exist apart from individuals.

28. The “minister” or agent is a basic concept in societies organized according to the organizational mode of association. The minister who carries out the will of God is a minister; the official who carries out the authority of the group is also a minister. Both are agents, not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and the minister, between the state and the government, must not be confused.

29. The social sphere that is pushed outward from the self is made up of one private connection after another, each strand being maintained by a moral element. The social sphere is extended from the self, and in this process of extension there are various routes. The most basic is kinship: parent-child ties and sibling ties, to which correspond the moral elements of filial piety and fraternal respect.

30. The parent-child tie is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. This social group is united for the bearing and rearing of children. In a society where the task of bearing and raising children is borne by individuals, such a group cannot be absent.
