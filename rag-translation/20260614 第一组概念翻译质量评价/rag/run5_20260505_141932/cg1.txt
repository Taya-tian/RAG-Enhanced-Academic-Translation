① Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a load. Each individual stick in the whole load belongs to a definite bundle, sheaf, and handful. Each stick can also find the other sticks in the same handful, the same sheaf, and the same bundle; the divisions are clear and do not become confused. In society, these units are groups. When I say that Western social organization is like bundled firewood, I mean to point out that it is often made up of groups formed by a number of people. A group has definite boundaries: who is inside the group and who is outside it cannot be left vague, but must be clearly distinguished. Those inside the group form one body, and their relation to the group is the same. If there are divisions or ranks within the same group, these too are defined in advance. My analogy of bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may join several groups, whereas it is of course impossible for the same stick to appear in several different sheaves. This is where men differ from sticks. I use the analogy only to make more concrete a certain pattern of relations among people in social life. We may call this the **group pattern**.

② In the West, the family is a group with clearly defined boundaries. If a friend writes to you saying that he is going to “bring his family” to visit you, he knows very well which people will be coming with him. In China, this phrase is extremely vague. In England and America, the family includes the man, his wife, and their minor children. If he were coming only with his wife, he would not use the word “family.” In China, although expressions such as “your whole household gracing us with your presence” are often seen, very few people could say exactly whom this “household” ought to include.

③ Speaking of our terms, the word **chia**—“family” or “house”—may be said to be the most elastic of all. “The one at home” may refer only to one’s own wife; “the family gate” may refer to a large group of uncles, cousins, nephews, and the like; “our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle in order to show intimacy. The range of “our own people” can expand or contract according to time and place; at its largest it is beyond counting—indeed, all under Heaven may become one family.

④ Why should the term for our most basic social unit be so unclear and ambiguous? In my view, this indicates that our social structure itself differs from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood tied up clearly one by one, but rather like the circles of ripples pushed outward when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water. Each person is the center of the circles pushed out by his social influence. Whoever is reached by the ripples of these circles comes into relation with him. The circle that each person mobilizes at a given time and place is not necessarily the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society have just this character of concentric ripples formed by throwing a stone into water. Kinship is the social relationship arising from the facts of birth and marriage. From the network formed by birth and marriage one can extend outward without limit, including people of the past, the present, and the future. Our common saying, “a cousinship can reach three thousand li,” expresses just this meaning; in fact, “three thousand li” merely indicates the breadth of its extension. This network is like a spider’s web: it has a center, namely oneself. Each of us has such a web spun out through kinship relations, but no two webs cover exactly the same people. People in a society may use the same system to identify their relatives; what is the same is only the system. The system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts of relationship. But when we use this system to identify concrete relatives, what each person identifies is different. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go one step further, there are no two people under Heaven whose recognized relatives can be completely identical. Two brothers may indeed have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Thus, as a network of social relations formed through kinship ties, it is individual and particular. Each network has an “I” as its center, and the center of every network is different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of territorial relations. The modern **pao-chia** system is patterned like a group organization, but this is quite incompatible with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each household takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around itself; this circle is the “neighborhood.” When there is a wedding or other happy event, neighbors are invited to drink; when a child is born, red eggs are sent; when there is a funeral, they come out to help with the encoffining and to carry the coffin. It is an institution of mutual aid in daily life. Yet it is not a fixed group, but a range. The size of the range depends on the power and influence of the center. The neighborhood of a powerful household may extend throughout the whole village; the neighborhood of a poor household may consist only of two or three adjacent families. This is the same as our kinship circles. In the Grand View Garden of the Chia family, for example, there could live Lin Tai-yü, a cousin on the father’s sister’s side, and Hsüeh Pao-ch’ai, a cousin on the mother’s sister’s side; later there were still more, such as Pao-ch’in and Hsiu-yen. As long as some kinship connection could be drawn, they could all be accommodated. But once the family’s power changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrank into a small cluster. In extreme cases, it could be like Su Ch’in returning home in destitution: “his wife did not treat him as a husband, and his sister-in-law did not treat him as an uncle.” The differential pattern in traditional Chinese structure has precisely this capacity to expand and contract. In the countryside, a family may be very small; but among wealthy landlords and the bureaucratic class, it may become as large as a small state. Chinese people are especially sensitive to the warmth and coldness of human relations precisely because these elastic social circles grow larger or smaller with changes in the power of the center.

⑦ In Western society, where even grown children living at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, everyone recognizes the boundaries of the group. Those inside the group possess definite qualifications. Once those qualifications are lost, they must leave the group. For them, this is not a question of the warmth or coldness of human feeling, but a question of rights. In Western society, what people struggle over is rights; in our society, it is connections and personal ties.
