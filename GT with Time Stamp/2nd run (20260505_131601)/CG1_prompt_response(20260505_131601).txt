**Group One: Chinese and Western Social Relations (Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”)**

① Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a shoulder-load. Each individual stalk, within the whole load, belongs to a definite bundle, sheaf, and handful. Each stalk can also be identified with the other stalks in the same handful, sheaf, or bundle; the divisions are clear and do not become confused. In society, these units are groups. When I say that Western social organization resembles bundled firewood, I mean to indicate that it is often composed of distinct groups formed by a number of persons. A group has definite boundaries: who belongs to the group and who stands outside it cannot be ambiguous, but must be clearly distinguished. Those within the group form one body, and their relation to the group is the same. If there are subdivisions or ranks within the same group, these too are stipulated in advance. The analogy of bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may belong to several groups, whereas it is of course impossible for one stalk of firewood to be present in several sheaves. This is where people differ from firewood. I use the metaphor in order to make more concrete a certain pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call this the **group pattern of association**.

② In the West, the family is a group with clearly defined boundaries. If a friend writes to you saying that he will “bring his family” to visit, he knows very well which persons will be coming with him. In China, by contrast, this expression is highly ambiguous. In Britain and America, “family” includes the man, his wife, and their minor children. If he were coming only with his wife, he would not use the word “family.” In China, although expressions such as “the whole household honoring us with their presence” are commonly seen, very few people can say exactly whom this “household” ought to include.

③ Speaking of our usage of words, the character **jia** 家 is perhaps the most flexible of all. “The person at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “family branch” may refer to a large number of uncles, cousins, and nephews; “one of our own” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s circle in order to express intimacy. The range of “our own people” expands and contracts according to time and circumstance; at its greatest extent it can become boundless—indeed, all under Heaven may become one family.

④ Why should the term for our most basic social unit be so vague and indeterminate? In my view, this indicates that the very structure of our society differs from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood tied up clearly one by one; rather, it resembles the ripples that spread outward in circles when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water. Each person is the center of the circles generated by his or her social influence. Those reached by the ripples of these circles enter into relation with that person. The circles that any individual mobilizes at a given time and place are not necessarily the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society have precisely this character of concentric ripples formed by the casting of a stone. Kinship relations are social relations arising from the facts of birth and marriage. The network formed through birth and marriage can be extended outward without limit, encompassing innumerable people—past, present, and future. The common saying “one cousinship extends three thousand li” conveys just this idea; “three thousand li,” of course, merely indicates vast extent. This network is like a spider’s web: it has a center, namely oneself. Each of us has such a web extending outward through kinship relations, but no two webs cover exactly the same people. People in a society may use the same system to identify their kin; what is shared is only this system. The system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts. When we use this system to identify concrete relatives, however, the people each person identifies are different. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people under Heaven whose recognized kin are completely identical. Two brothers may indeed have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Thus, as a network of social relations linked by kinship, it is particular to each individual. Each network has an “I” or “self” at its center, and the centers of the various networks are all different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of locality-based relations. The modern **baojia** system is organized according to the group pattern, but it is fundamentally incompatible with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each household takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around it; this circle is the “neighborhood.” When there is a wedding, neighbors are invited to drink; when a child is born, red eggs are sent; when there is a funeral, they come out to help enshroud the body and carry the coffin. It is an institution of mutual aid in daily life. Yet it is not a fixed group, but a range. The size of this range depends on the strength or weakness of the center’s influence. The neighborhood of a powerful household may extend throughout the entire village, whereas that of a poor household may include only two or three adjacent families. This is the same as our kinship circles. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, for instance, there could live Lin Daiyu, a paternal cousin, and Xue Baochai, a maternal cousin; later still more people were added—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. Anyone who could be connected by kinship could be accommodated. But once power changes, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle contracts into a small cluster. In extreme cases, it may be like Su Qin returning home in poverty: “his wife did not treat him as a husband, and his sister-in-law did not treat him as an uncle.” The differential mode of association in China’s traditional structure possesses precisely this capacity for expansion and contraction. In the countryside, a family may be very small; yet among wealthy landlords and officials, it may become as large as a small state. Chinese people are especially sensitive to the fickleness of human relations precisely because these flexible social circles expand or contract with changes in the power of the center.

⑦ In Western society, where even grown children who continue to live at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, everyone recognizes the boundaries of the group. Those inside the group possess definite qualifications for membership. Once those qualifications are lost, one must leave the group. For them, this is not a matter of the warmth or coldness of personal feeling, but a matter of rights. In Western society, people contend over rights; in ours, people seek connections and appeal to personal ties.
