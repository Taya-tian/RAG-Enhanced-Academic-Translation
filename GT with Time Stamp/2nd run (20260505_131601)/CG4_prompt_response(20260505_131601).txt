**Group Four: Chinese and Western Forms of the Family**  
**Chapter Six: “The Family/Lineage”**

① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, a group ordinarily referred to as the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China*, I called it the “expanded family.” In both expressions, the core term is “family,” to which a modifier such as “small” or “large” is added in order to indicate a formal difference between Chinese and Western families that are assumed to be essentially of the same nature. Yet it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group of Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the difference between the Chinese and Western “family” from the standpoint of structural principles. The distinction commonly made between the large family and the small family is by no means a distinction of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but rather a distinction of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby satisfy the conditions of a “large family,” while a household consisting only of parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—four persons in all—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles on which the two are organized are different.

③ In anthropology, the concept of the family has a definite definition: it is a procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born to the married couple. The purpose for which this group is formed is the birth and nurture of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is undertaken by individuals, such a group is indispensable. Yet the procreative function, when considered in relation to each individual family, is temporary. Once children have grown up, they leave the nurture of their parents and go on to manage the business of bearing and rearing children of their own, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not entirely like ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary groups; although in fact they are not eternal, they are not provisional, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the very beginning prepare for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring the period of nurture to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not cease when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to the group constituted by parents and children, then both at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up there is a period in which it consists only of the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do indeed conduct economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation between themselves, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Any undertaking requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be managed by some other social group.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the range of affairs this group can manage is quite limited, the principal one being the procreation and rearing of children. In Chinese rural society, however, the *jia* has no strict corporate boundaries. Its members may expand outward as need requires along the graded sequence of kinship relations. The persons who make up what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet structurally, the line along which expansion takes place is restricted. The Chinese *jia* expands along a unilineal path: it includes only the paternal line. Except in a few cases, the *jia* cannot simultaneously include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are persons of another family. On the father’s side, however, expansion may extend very far. A household of five generations living under one roof may include all paternal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to this principle of unilineal kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our *jia* is a clan. Yet it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a *zu*, or lineage/clan, because what we call a *zu* is composed of many *jia*; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are identical in structural principle; they differ only in quantity or size. This is why I am unwilling to use the term “large family” and instead use “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation—the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended outward from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring within the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it constitutes an independent unit in its own right; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying out undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our *jia* is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopts the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and carry out various undertakings, thereby causing the basic *jia* to take on the character of a clan. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry out so many undertakings, the structure of the *jia* cannot be limited to the small grouping of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group cannot be temporary in the way the Western family is. The *jia* must be continuous: it must not split apart when individuals mature, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the *jia* becomes that of the lineage. The clan is by nature long-term, just as our *jia* is. To call this kind of social group a “small lineage” also expresses this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese *jia* is an organization for conducting undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the *jia* may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife alone can bear, then brothers, uncles, and other paternal relatives may all gather within one large *jia*. This explains why the size of the *jia* in our rural society may vary greatly. Yet no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to assume many other functions, the content of the relationships among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly manage the affairs of procreation and child-rearing; children play a secondary role in this group, and once they have grown up they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Since husband and wife form the main axis, the emotional bond between the sexes becomes the cohesive force. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes the family the center from which people obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the *jia* differs markedly in this respect. Since our *jia* is a continuous organization for carrying out undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. It is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are compelled by the needs of the undertaking to exclude ordinary feelings. By “ordinary feelings,” I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To pursue efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private sentiment. In the Chinese family there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of a social group organized for the conduct of undertakings.
