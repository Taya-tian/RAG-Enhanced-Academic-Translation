① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” The main term in these names is “family”; an adjective such as “small” or “large” is added to indicate a formal difference between the Chinese and Western “family,” which is assumed to be the same in nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small clan.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the principle of structure, the difference between the Chinese and Western forms of the “family.” The distinction between what we commonly call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large family”; whereas a household of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved are different.

③ In anthropology the concept of the family has a clear definition: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother, and the children are limited to those born of the spouses. This group is united for the bearing and rearing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is undertaken by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the function of procreation, for each individual family, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the nurture of their parents and go on to manage the affairs of bearing and rearing their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states are not temporary groups; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, for their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the beginning prepare for its own conclusion. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring the rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to the group formed by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and again after the children have grown up there is a period when it is merely the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do indeed carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the range of affairs they can undertake is greatly limited. Any undertaking requiring the cooperation of a larger number of persons must be carried on by some other social group.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs that this group can undertake are few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members constituting what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which the structure expands is restricted. The Chinese chia expands along a unilineal line; that is, it includes only the paternal side. Except in a few cases, the chia cannot at the same time include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are both people of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, the expansion may go very far. A household of five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed on the basis of the principle of unilineal kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our chia is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a lineage or clan, because what we call a clan is composed of many chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason I propose here the term “small clan.” The small clan and the large clan are the same in structural principle; their difference lies in quantity, in size. This is why I do not wish to use the term “large family,” but instead use “small clan.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the clan includes the family; the smallest clan may even be equal to a family. For the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The clan is extended from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the clan is only one ring in a social circle. We cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can we say that it forms an independent unit by itself. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the clan includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on affairs; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the moral order of kinship to organize social groups and carry on various undertakings, thereby making the basic chia clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the clan. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be enlarged. Moreover, all political, economic, and religious affairs require long-term continuity. This basic social group absolutely cannot be temporary like the Western family. The chia must be continuous: it must not split apart because an individual grows up, nor come to an end because an individual dies. Thus the nature of the chia becomes that of the clan. The clan is by nature long-term, just as our chia is. To call this kind of social group a “small clan” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast with the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese chia is an organization for carrying on undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be enough, and the chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, brothers and paternal uncles may all be gathered into one large chia. This explains why the size of the chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly carry on the affairs of procreation, while the children play supporting roles in the group; when they grow up, they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Since husband and wife become the main axis, affection between the sexes is the force of cohesion. The development of sexual and emotional feeling makes the family the center from which comfort in life is obtained. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our chia is a continuous group for carrying on undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are, under the demands of the undertaking, made to exclude ordinary affection. By “ordinary affection” I mean that which stands in contrast to discipline. No undertaking can be separated from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are subject to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; and between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of a social group organized for carrying on undertakings.
