① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “Expanded Family.” In these terms, the principal word is “family,” and an adjective such as “small” or “large” is added to indicate the formal difference between the Chinese and Western “family,” which are assumed to be the same in nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia from the standpoint of structural principle. The difference between what we ordinarily call the large-family and the small-family is certainly not a difference in size, not a difference in the number of persons included in the group, but a difference in structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby satisfy the conditions of a “large-family,” while a Chia consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small-family.” In number, the former is larger than the latter; but in structure, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles on which the two are based are different.

③ The concept of the family has a definite definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married pair. The group is formed for the birth and rearing of children. In a society in which the task of bearing and rearing children is assumed by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, in the case of each individual family, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to undertake the procreation and rearing of their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states are not temporary groups; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must, from the very beginning, prepare for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring the rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Procreation*.

④ Yet in every culture the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to the group constituted by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is merely the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation; but the affairs they can manage are greatly restricted. All undertakings that require the cooperation of a larger number of people must be carried on by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate group; as I have explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia does not have strict corporate boundaries. The members of this group may, according to need, extend outward along the differential order of kinship. The members constituting what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. But the line along which it expands is restricted in structure. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line; that is, it includes only the paternal side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are both people of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, expansion may go very far. A Chia in which five generations live together may include all paternal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed according to this unilineal kinship principle has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a lineage, because what we call a lineage is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity, or size. This is why I do not wish to use “large-family,” but instead use “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ In structure, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equal to the family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended outward from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in the social circle. We cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can we say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to the function of procreation. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for undertaking affairs; if further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions may all be borne by the lineage; on the other hand, we may also say that, in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children, but must be expanded. Moreover, all political, economic, and religious affairs require long-term continuity; this basic social group can by no means be temporary, as the Western family is. The Chia must be continuous: it must not divide because an individual grows up, nor come to an end because an individual dies. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a lineage. The clan is by nature long-lasting, just like our Chia. To call this social group of ours a “small lineage” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast with the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for undertaking affairs, and the size of the Chia is determined by the size of the undertaking. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as the family. If the undertaking is large, exceeding what husband and wife can bear, then brothers, uncles, and nephews may all gather into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, patrilineal, differential mode.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family group, husband and wife are the main axis. Husband and wife jointly carry on the task of procreation; the children play a secondary role in this group, and when they have grown up they leave it. Among them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the province of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force that binds the group together. The development of sexual affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑪ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for undertaking affairs, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a subsidiary axis. Although this subsidiary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are compelled by the needs of the undertaking to exclude ordinary feelings. By ordinary feelings I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can be separated from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private sentiment. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are stressed. All these are characteristics of a social group organized for undertaking affairs.
