**Group Two: Chinese and Western Egocentrism and Individualism  
(Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”)**

① With the “self” at the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations one forms with others are not like the members of a group all standing on the same plane; rather, they are like ripples on the surface of water, spreading outward circle by circle—the farther they spread, the more distant and the thinner they become. Here we encounter the basic characteristic of Chinese social structure. What Confucianism pays the greatest attention to is *renlun*—human relations. What is *lun*? My explanation is that it refers to the differential order of the successive ripples that arise among those people who, spreading outward from oneself, enter into social relations with oneself. The *Shiming* also says under the character *lun* 沦: “It is *lun* 伦; the patterns of water follow one another and have order.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once said that all meanings with *lun* 仑 as their common component are connected; “what they jointly express is a sense of arrangement, category, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The “ten *lun*” discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—spirits and men, ruler and ministers, father and sons, noble and base, close and distant kin, ranks and rewards, husband and wife, government affairs, elders and juniors, superiors and inferiors—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing between father and son, near and far, close and distant kin. *Lun* is an order with gradations. To us today, how can concrete social relations such as spirits and men, ruler and ministers, father and sons, husband and wife be placed on the same level as abstract relative positions such as noble and base, close and distant, near and far, superior and inferior? In fact, in our traditional social structure, the most basic concept—the guiding order in the network formed by human association—is precisely a differential order, that is, *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: “To treat kin as kin, to honor the honorable, to respect elders as elders, and to maintain distinctions between men and women—these are things that cannot be changed along with the people.” The meaning is that the framework of this social structure cannot be changed; what changes are only the affairs carried out by making use of this framework.

③ What Confucius paid the greatest attention to was the word “extend,” as in the outward expansion of ripples on water. He first acknowledged a self, a self from which one extends to others. This self must be restrained by ritual; to restrain the self is to cultivate the person. Following the concentric circles of human relations, one may then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “A man who is filial and fraternal yet likes to offend his superiors is rare; one who does not like to offend his superiors yet likes to create disorder has never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* takes the five relations as “the universal Way of all under Heaven.” Because in this kind of social structure one extends outward circle by circle from the self to all under Heaven, Mencius said that he was merely “good at extending.”

④ In this highly elastic network, there is always, at every time and place, a “self” as the center. This is not individualism, but egocentrism. The individual is spoken of in relation to the group; it is the member in relation to the group. Under individualism, on the one hand there is the idea of equality, meaning that the positions of all members within the same group are equal and that the individual may not infringe upon the rights of the group; on the other hand there is the idea of constitutionalism, meaning that the group may not obliterate the individual and may control the individual only over that portion of rights which the individual is willing to surrender. These ideas must first presuppose the existence of a group. In our traditional Chinese thought there is no such set of ideas, because what we have is egocentrism: a doctrine in which all values take the “self” as their center.

⑤ Egocentrism is not limited to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair to benefit all under Heaven; even Confucianism should be included within it. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu neglected the relativity and elasticity of egocentrism. He was too rigid-minded, clinging stubbornly to the self and refusing to let go. Confucius, by contrast, knew how to extend from himself to others. Yet even if this extension reached to the four seas, the center still remained the self. The Master said: “To govern by virtue is like the North Star: it remains in its place, and the multitude of stars revolve around it.” This is an excellent metaphor for the differential mode of association: the self is always the center, like the unmoving pole star, while all others turn around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus had a collectivity beyond the individual—he had his Kingdom of Heaven—so he could sacrifice himself to fulfill the Kingdom of Heaven. Confucius was different.

⑥ Zigong said: “Suppose there were one who broadly bestowed benefits upon the people and was able to aid the multitude—what of him? Could he be called humane?” The Master said: “Why speak only of humaneness? He would surely be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would have found this difficult. As for the humane person: wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to attain success himself, he helps others attain success. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy—this may be called the method of humaneness.”

⑦ In Confucius’s moral system, he never departs from the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks it in himself; the petty man seeks it in others.” Therefore, he could not, like Jesus, love all under Heaven universally, even loving his enemies and asking God to forgive those who killed him—these things do not proceed from the self as center. What of Confucius? Someone asked: “What do you think of repaying resentment with virtue?” The Master said: “Then with what will you repay virtue? Repay resentment with uprightness, and repay virtue with virtue.” This is a matter of differential levels, and Confucius would never relax them. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, use the small self to deal with every situation; rather, he expanded or contracted the moral sphere according to need. He was not like Jesus, or like Mo Di in China, who once they extended it outward could no longer draw it back.
