1. Western society is somewhat like our bundling firewood in the fields: a few stalks of straw are tied into a handful, a few handfuls into a bundle, a few bundles into a sheaf, and a few sheaves into a carrying load.

2. In China, this expression is extremely ambiguous.

3. This network is like a spider’s web: it has a center, namely oneself.

4. The idea of the “citizen” presupposes the prior existence of a “state.”

5. A group of people dependent upon one another for their livelihood cannot survive singly and scattered in the mountains and forests.

6. The concept of a constitution corresponds to the Western concept of public office.

7. The union of this social group is for the birth and upbringing of children.

8. On the father’s side, however, it can be extended very far. A Chia with five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the two sexes is the force of cohesion.

10. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. “Those at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “members of the family house” may refer to a large group including uncles, cousins, and nephews; “one’s own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle in order to show intimacy. The scope of “one’s own people” is flexible according to time and place; it may expand so widely as to become countless—indeed, all under heaven may become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I call the “differential mode of association,” a “network formed by one private tie after another.” This mode differs from the modern Western “organizational mode of association.”

13. But as I now see it, this term is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

14. Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to the family. This is because the foundation of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, the clan is an enterprise organization; when further expanded, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical principles of kinship to organize social groups and conduct various enterprises, so that the basic Chia becomes clan-like.

17. The Chinese Chia is an enterprise organization, and its size is determined by the scale of the enterprise.

18. Since our Chia is a continuous enterprise group, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal.

19. I use this metaphor in order to make more concrete the pattern of relationships among people in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system belongs to the organizational mode of association, but it is utterly incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward indefinitely to include an infinite number of people—past, present, and future. The popular saying “a maternal cousin three thousand li away” expresses precisely this meaning; indeed, the “three thousand li” merely indicates its great extent.

22. In the kinship system, we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized kin can be exactly the same. Two brothers, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is just like our kinship circle. In the Grand View Garden of the Chia family, for example, there could live Lin Daiyu, a paternal cousin through an aunt, and Xue Baochai, a maternal cousin through an aunt; later there were even more, such as Baoqin and Xiuyan. Anyone who could be connected as kin could be accommodated. But once the family’s power changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrank into a small group.

24. Taking the “self” as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like members of an organization standing on the same plane. Rather, they are like ripples on the surface of water, spreading outward circle by circle; the farther they spread, the more distant and thinner they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The ten *lun* discussed in the “Ji Tong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—between ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, close and distant kin, rank and reward, husband and wife, government affairs, elder and younger, and superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, close and distant kin. *Lun* is an order of graded distinctions.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “Few are those who are filial and fraternal yet fond of offending their superiors; none has ever been disinclined to offend superiors yet fond of causing disorder.” From the self to the Chia, from the Chia to the state, and from the state to all under heaven—this is a continuous path. In the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the five relationships are treated as “the universal Way of all under heaven.”

27. An organization is a “reality” transcending the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot concretely produce some visible object and say that this is the organization. It is a bundle of relations among people, a force that controls the behavior of each individual, an object upon which the lives of its members depend, a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot exist apart from individuals…

28. The “agent,” or Minister, is a fundamental concept in a society based on the organizational mode of association. The pastor who carries out the will of God is a Minister; the official who carries out the power of the organization is also a Minister. Both are “agents,” not God or the organization itself. The distinction between God and the pastor, and between the state and the government, must not be confused.

29. The social sphere constructed by extending outward from the self consists of one private tie after another, each tie being maintained by a moral element. The social sphere is pushed outward from the “self,” and in the process of extension there are various routes. The most fundamental is kinship: parent-child and sibling relations, whose corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal duty.

30. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. The union of this social group is for the birth and upbringing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a group will never be lacking.
