① Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a bundle, several bundles into a sheaf, and several sheaves into a shoulder-load. Every single stalk in the whole load belongs to a definite sheaf, bundle, and handful. Each stalk can also find the other stalks in the same handful, bundle, and sheaf; the divisions are clear and will not be confused. In society, these units are groups. When I say that Western social organization is like bundled firewood, I mean to point out that it is commonly made up of groups formed by a number of people. A group has definite boundaries: who is inside the group and who is outside it cannot be left vague, but must be clearly distinguished. Those inside the group are one body; their relation to the group is the same. If within the same group there are subdivisions or ranks, these too are stipulated in advance. My analogy of bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may take part in several groups, whereas it is of course impossible for the same stalk of firewood to appear in several bundles. This is where people differ from firewood. I use the metaphor only to make more concrete a certain pattern of relationships among people in social life. We may call it the group mode of association.

② In the West, the family is a group with clearly defined boundaries. If a friend writes to say that he is going to “bring his family” to visit you, he knows perfectly well which people will be coming with him. In China, such a phrase is extremely ambiguous. In England and America, the family includes the man himself, his wife, and his minor children. If he came only with his wife, he would not use the word “family.” In China, although the polite phrase “may your whole household honor us with your presence” is often seen, very few people could say exactly who this “household” ought to include.

③ Speaking of our terminology, the word *chia*—“family” or “house”—is perhaps the most elastic of all. “The one at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “members of the house” may refer to a large group of uncles, cousins, and nephews; “our own people” may include anyone one wishes to draw into one’s own circle as a sign of intimacy. The range of “our own people” can expand or contract according to time and place. It may become so large that it cannot be counted; truly, all under heaven may become one family.

④ Why should the term for our most basic social unit be so unclear? In my view, this shows that our social structure itself is different from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood, clearly tied together one by one, but rather like the ripples formed when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water, spreading outward in circle after circle. Each person is the center of the circles produced by his own social influence. Whoever is reached by the ripples of these circles enters into relation with him. The circle a person makes use of at any given time and place is not always the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society have precisely this character of concentric ripples formed by throwing a stone into water. Kinship relations are social relations arising from the facts of birth and marriage. The network formed through birth and marriage can be extended outward without limit, including people of the past, the present, and the future. Our common saying that “a cousinship may extend for three thousand *li*” expresses just this idea; in fact, “three thousand *li*” merely indicates great breadth. This network is like a spider’s web, with a center, namely oneself. Each of us has such a web spread out through kinship relations, but no two webs cover exactly the same people. People in the same society may use the same system to identify their relatives; what is shared is only the system. The system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts of relationship. But when we use this system to identify concrete relatives, the persons identified differ from individual to individual. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. Going further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized relatives can be completely identical. Two brothers do of course have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Thus, as a network of social relations linked through kinship, it is individual. Each network has a “self” as its center, and the center of each network is different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of territorial relations. The modern *pao-chia* system belongs to the group mode of association, but this is entirely at odds with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each household takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around itself; this circle is the “neighborhood.” When there is a wedding, neighbors are invited to drink wine; when a child is born, red eggs are sent; when there is a death, they come out to help prepare the body and carry the coffin. It is an institution of mutual aid in daily life. Yet it is not a fixed group, but a range. The size of this range depends on the strength and influence of the center. The neighborhood of a powerful household may extend over the whole village; that of a poor household may include only two or three adjacent families. This is just like our kinship circle. In the Grand View Garden of the Chia family, there could live Lin Tai-yü, a cousin through a paternal aunt, and Hsüeh Pao-ch’ai, a cousin through a maternal aunt; later there were still more, such as Pao-ch’in and Hsiu-yen. Anyone who could be drawn into some kinship connection could be accommodated. But once power changes, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrinks into a small cluster. In the extreme case, it may be like Su Ch’in returning home in poverty: “his wife did not regard him as a husband, and his sister-in-law did not regard him as a brother-in-law.” The differential mode of association in China’s traditional structure has this capacity to expand and contract. In the countryside, the family may be very small; but among wealthy landlords and bureaucrats, it may grow as large as a small state. Chinese people are especially sensitive to the warmth and coldness of human relations precisely because these elastic social circles change in size according to changes in the power of their center.

⑦ In Western society, where even grown children living at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, people recognize the boundaries of the group. Those inside the group have definite qualifications. Once these qualifications are lost, they must leave the group. For them, this is not a matter of the warmth or coldness of human feeling, but a matter of rights. In Western society, people struggle over rights; among us, people seek to draw connections and cultivate personal ties.
