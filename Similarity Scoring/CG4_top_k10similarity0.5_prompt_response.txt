1. What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “expanded family.” In these terms the main word is “family,” with a modifying adjective such as small or large added to it in order to indicate formal differences between what are assumed to be families of the same nature in China and in the West. But it now seems to me that this is not appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be “small lineage.”

2. My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, in terms of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia. The difference between what we commonly call the large-family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but a matter of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions for being a “large-family,” while a Chia consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” In numbers the former is larger than the latter, but in structure the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved are different.

3. The concept of the family has a clear definition in anthropology: it is a procreative social group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. This social group is united for the bearing and rearing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, for each individual family, is short-term. Once the children have grown up, they leave the nurture of their parents and go on to manage the procreation and rearing of their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states are not permanent in fact, but neither are they temporary, for the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must prepare for its own ending from the very beginning. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Procreation*.

4. Yet in every culture the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change in nature, and remains limited to the social group constituted by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is only the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife certainly carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they manage are greatly limited. Affairs requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be managed by other social groups.

5. In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who constitute what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which it expands structurally is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line, including only the paternal side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are both people of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, expansion can go very far. A Chia with five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

6. This kind of social group, organized according to the principle of unilineal kinship, has a technical name in anthropology: the clan. In structure, our Chia is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a lineage, because what we call a lineage is composed of many Chia; it is a social group of social groups. For this reason I introduce here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity, or size. This is why I do not wish to use “large-family,” but instead use “small lineage.” The difference of one word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

7. In structure, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended outward from the foundation of the family. But the family included in the lineage is only one ring within a social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own. It is not a corporate group.

8. Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for joint undertakings; expanded further, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex functions—political, economic, religious, and so on. Our Chia is exactly like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopts the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like in character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions may all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that, in order to manage so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small grouping of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group cannot, like the Western family, be temporary. The Chia must be continuous; it must not split apart because individuals grow up, nor end because individuals die. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a clan. The clan is by nature long-term, just as our Chia is. To call this kind of social group a small lineage also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

9. The Chinese Chia is an organization for joint undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of the undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife is sufficient to handle it, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, then brothers, uncles, and nephews may all gather into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the differences in size, the structural principle remains consistent: a unilineal differential mode of association.

10. When a procreative social group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife are the main axis. Husband and wife jointly manage the affairs of procreation; children play a secondary role in the group, and when they grow up they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups; they do not fall within the family’s sphere. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affections between the sexes are the force of cohesion. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes their family the center for obtaining comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous group for joint undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are such that ordinary feelings are excluded by the needs of the undertaking. By “ordinary feelings” I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can be separated from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feelings. In the Chinese family there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of a social group organized for joint undertakings.
