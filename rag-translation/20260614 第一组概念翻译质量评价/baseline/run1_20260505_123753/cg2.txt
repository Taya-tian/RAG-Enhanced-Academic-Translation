① Taking the “self” as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relationships one forms with others are not like the members of a group all standing on the same plane. Rather, they resemble ripples on the surface of water: circle after circle spreads outward, and the farther they extend, the weaker they become. Here we encounter the fundamental characteristic of Chinese social structure. What Confucianism has examined most carefully is *renlun*—human relations. What, then, is *lun*? My explanation is that it refers to the differential ordering of the successive circles of ripples that arise among those people who, extending outward from oneself, enter into social relations with oneself. The *Shiming* also says under the graph *lun* 沦: “*Lun* means ordered relations; the patterns of water follow one another and thus have order.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once said that all meanings sharing *lun* 仑 as a common component are interconnected; “together they express the ideas of pattern, category, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The ten *lun* discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—between spirits and humans, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, intimate and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, affairs of government, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to differences of degree. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order of graded distinctions. To us today, how could such concrete social relations as spirits and humans, ruler and minister, father and son, or husband and wife be placed on the same level as such abstract relative statuses as noble and base, intimate and distant, near and far, or superior and inferior? In fact, within our traditional social structure, the most basic concept—the guiding order within the network formed by interpersonal dealings—is precisely a differential order, that is, *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: “Loving one’s kin as kin, honoring the honorable, treating elders as elders, and maintaining distinctions between men and women—these are things that cannot be changed by the people.” The meaning is that the framework of this social structure cannot be altered; what changes are merely the things done by making use of this framework.

③ What Confucius emphasized most was the word “extend” (*tui*)—the outward expansion of the ripples. He first acknowledged a “self,” the self from which one extends oneself to others. This self must be restrained by ritual propriety; to restrain the self is to cultivate the person. Following the ethical order of these concentric circles, one can then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “Those who are filial and fraternal yet fond of offending their superiors are few; those who are not fond of offending their superiors yet fond of creating disorder have never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the Five Relations as “the universal Way of all under Heaven.” Because, in this kind of social structure, the movement from self to all under Heaven proceeds outward circle by circle, Mencius said of himself that he was simply “good at extending” what was near at hand.

④ In this highly elastic network, there is always and everywhere a “self” serving as the center. This is not individualism, but egocentrism. The individual is spoken of in relation to the group; it is the member in relation to the collectivity. Under individualism, on the one hand there is the idea of equality, meaning that all members within the same group occupy equal positions and that no individual may infringe upon the rights of the whole; on the other hand there is the idea of constitutionalism, meaning that the group may not obliterate the individual and may control the individual only over that portion of rights which the individual is willing to surrender. These ideas must first presuppose the existence of the group. In traditional Chinese thought, we do not have this set of assumptions, because what we have is egocentrism: a doctrine in which all values take the “self” as their center.

⑤ Egocentrism is not limited to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair to benefit the whole world; even Confucianism should be included within it. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu ignored the relativity and elasticity of egocentrism. He was too rigid, clinging stubbornly to the self and refusing to let go. Confucius, by contrast, knew how to extend the self to others; yet even if this extension reached to the four seas, the center still remained the self. The Master said: “To govern by virtue is like the Pole Star: it remains in its place, and the multitude of stars revolve around it.” This is an excellent metaphor for the differential mode of association. The self is always the center, like the Pole Star, unmoving through the seasons, while all other people revolve around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus possessed a collectivity transcending the individual—he had his Kingdom of Heaven—and therefore he could sacrifice himself in order to fulfill that Kingdom. Confucius was not so.

⑥ Zigong said: “If there were someone who broadly conferred benefits upon the people and was able to aid the multitude, what would you say of him? Could he be called benevolent?” The Master said: “Why stop at benevolence? Such a person would surely be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would have found this difficult. As for the benevolent person: wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to attain success himself, he enables others to attain success. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy may be called the method of benevolence.”

⑦ In Confucius’s moral system, he never allowed himself to depart from the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks it in himself; the petty man seeks it in others.” For this reason, he could not, like Jesus, love all under Heaven universally, even loving his enemies and praying to God to forgive those who killed him—such acts do not proceed from a self-centered point of departure. What about Confucius? Someone asked: “What do you think of repaying resentment with virtue?” The Master said: “Then with what would one repay virtue? Repay resentment with uprightness, and repay virtue with virtue.” This is a hierarchy of differential levels, and Confucius never relinquished it. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, use the narrow self to respond to every situation; rather, he expanded or contracted the scope of morality according to need. He was not like Jesus or China’s Mo Di, who, once they extended it outward, could no longer draw it back.
