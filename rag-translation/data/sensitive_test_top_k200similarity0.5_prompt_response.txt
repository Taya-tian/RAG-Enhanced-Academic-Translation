4. The concept of the “citizen” cannot exist without first having the concept of the “state.”
5. A group of people whose lives are mutually dependent cannot survive separately and in scattered fashion in the mountains and forests.
6. The constitutional idea corresponds to the Western conception of public office.
7. This community is united for the birth and upbringing of children.
8. On the father’s side, however, it may be extended very far; a household in which five generations live together may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.
9. Husband and wife become the central axis, and affection between the sexes is the cohesive force.
10. In my book American Character I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.
11. “Those of the household” may refer only to one’s own wife; “the family gate” may include uncles, cousins, and nephews in great numbers; “our own people” may embrace anyone drawn into one’s own circle as a sign of intimacy. The range of “our own people” expands and contracts according to time and place. It can become so vast as to be beyond counting—indeed, all under Heaven may become one family.
12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I call the “differential mode of association,” a “network composed of personal relationships.” This pattern differs from the “organizational mode” of modern Western society.
13. Yet I now feel that this term is not quite appropriate. A more accurate designation for the basic social group of Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage.”
14. Structurally, the lineage includes the family, and the smallest lineage may be equivalent to a family. For the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child.
15. According to anthropological terminology, a clan is an organizational unit for common enterprise, and when enlarged further it may become a tribe.
16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association and used kinship ethics to organize communities and manage various enterprises, thereby transforming the basic family into something clan-like.
17. The Chinese family is an organizational unit for enterprise, and the size of the family is determined by the scale of the enterprise.
18. Since our family is a continuous community of enterprise, its main axis lies between father and son, between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical rather than horizontal.
19. I use this metaphor in order to make more concrete the pattern of human relationships in social life. We may call this the organizational mode.
20. The modern pao-chia system is organizational in character, but it is completely incompatible with the traditional structure.
21. The network formed through reproduction and marriage can be extended indefinitely to include an infinite number of persons—past, present, and future. In our common saying, “a cousinship extends three thousand li,” the “three thousand li” merely signifies vast extension.
22. Within the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. Furthermore, no two persons can recognize exactly the same set of relatives. Two brothers, to be sure, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.
23. This is much like our kinship circles. In the Grand View Garden of the Chia family, cousins such as Lin Tai-yu and Hsueh Pao-ch’ai could reside together; later there were still more—Pao-ch’in, Hsiu-yen, and anyone who could claim kinship could be accommodated. But once the family power declined, the tree fell and the monkeys scattered, shrinking into a tiny cluster.
24. Taking the self as the center, like a stone dropped into water, the social relationships connected with others are not like members standing together on the same plane in an organization; rather, they are like ripples spreading outward in circles, becoming more distant and thinner as they expand.
25. Ethics emphasize distinctions. In the Book of Rites, the “ten relationships” — spirits and gods, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and humble, near and distant kin, rank and reward, husband and wife, affairs of government, elder and younger, upper and lower — all refer to gradations. “Not losing one’s proper relation” means distinguishing father and son, distance and nearness, intimacy and remoteness. Relations are ordered according to degrees of difference.
26. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “Rare indeed is the man who is filial and fraternal yet fond of offending his superiors; there has never been one who dislikes offending his superiors yet delights in rebellion.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, from the state to all under Heaven, there is a single path. The Doctrine of the Mean regards the five cardinal relationships as the “universal Way under Heaven.”
27. An organization is a “reality” transcending the individual, though not a tangible thing. We cannot point to some physical object and say, “This is the organization.” It is a bundle of human relationships, a force controlling individual conduct, an object upon which the lives of its members depend, a common will prior to any individual yet inseparable from individuals themselves.
28. The “minister” as representative is a basic concept in a society organized according to the organizational mode. The priest who executes the will of God is a minister; the official who executes the power of the organization is also a minister. Both are “agents,” not God or the organization itself. The distinction between God and priest, between state and government, must not be confused.
29. The social sphere extending outward from the self is composed of personal ties, each maintained by a moral element. Society radiates outward from the self along various routes, the most fundamental being kinship — parent-child and siblings — with the corresponding moral elements of filial piety and brotherly respect.
30. The parent-child relationship is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the marital union. This community is united for the birth and upbringing of children. In a society where individuals themselves bear responsibility for childbearing and rearing, such a community cannot be absent.
