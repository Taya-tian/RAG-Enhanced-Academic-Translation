**Group Three: Moral Concepts Arising from Differences in Chinese and Western Social Structures  
(Chapter 5, “The Morality That Maintains Private Relationships”)**

① The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I have called a “differential mode of association,” a “network formed by strand after strand of personal connections.” This pattern is different from the modern Western “organizational mode of association.” In the organizational mode, the ties among individuals depend upon a common framework: first there is this framework, and then each person is attached to it and thereby enters into relations with others. The concept of the “citizen” necessarily presupposes the prior existence of a “state.” This structure may well have been handed down from the tribal forms of primitive peoples. In a nomadic economy, the tribal form quite clearly displays an “organizational mode of association.” A group of people whose lives depend upon one another cannot survive singly and scattered through mountains and forests. For them, the “group” is the precondition of life. But in a settled rural society, where each person can live by his own labor on the land, the need for companions is felt only in accidental, temporary, or extraordinary circumstances. For such people, relations with others are secondary and derivative; moreover, in different situations they require different degrees of association, and they do not have a marked need for a permanent and widely extended group. Therefore their society takes the form of a “differential mode of association.”

② Differences in the pattern of social structure give rise to different moral concepts. Moral concepts are the beliefs held by people living in society that they ought consciously to observe the norms of social conduct. They include norms of behavior, the convictions of the actor, and the sanctions of society. Their content consists of the rules governing relations between persons, and is determined by the pattern of that society. From the social point of view, morality is the force by which society sanctions individual conduct, making people act in accordance with prescribed forms in order to maintain the survival and continuity of that society.

③ In the “organizational mode of association,” the basic moral concepts are built upon the relation between the group and the individual. The group is a “reality” that transcends the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot concretely point to some physical object and say: this is the group. It is a bundle of relations among people, a force that controls the conduct of each individual, an object upon which the lives of its component members depend, a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot exist apart from individuals. Such a “reality” can only be symbolized and represented by something tangible. It is only in societies with an “organizational mode of association” that the idea of an all-embracing God arises. The relation of the group to the individual is symbolized in the relation of God to the believer: He is a judge who rewards and punishes, an impartial maintainer of order, and an omnipotent protector.

④ If we wish to understand the moral system of Western societies with their “organizational mode of association,” we absolutely cannot separate it from their religious ideas. Religious devotion and trust are not only the source of their moral concepts, but also the force that supports their norms of conduct; religion is the symbol of the group. Under the concept of God as the symbol of the group, two important derivative ideas appear: first, that every individual is equal before God; second, that God is just toward every individual.

⑤ Jesus called God the Father, a father common to every person; he even publicly denied the parents who had given birth to him. In order to carry through this “equality,” Christian mythology has Jesus born of a virgin. Here the particular and private tie between parent and child is negated. In fact, this is not “nonsense”; it is a powerful symbol. It symbolizes the “public” group: the representative of the group—God—must be without private interest. Every “son of man,” the “component member of the group” symbolized by Jesus, must have, beyond his private father, a more important father shared with others: the “Heavenly Father,” that is, the group. Only in this way can equality in each individual’s personhood be established, for the relation between every member of the group and the group itself is equal. The group cannot be privately possessed by any individual. It is on this basis that the American *Declaration of Independence* begins with the words: “All men are created equal; they are endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable rights.”

⑥ But God exists in the unseen realm, precisely symbolizing the invisible reality of the group. Yet when the will of the group is to be carried out, someone must act on its behalf. The “agent” or *minister* is a basic concept in societies with an organizational mode of association. The pastor who carries out the will of God is a minister; the official who carries out the power of the group is also a minister. Both are “agents,” not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and the pastor, between the state and the government, must not be confused. In the history of Christianity, people again and again demanded direct communion with God and resisted the “agents” who could not truly represent God’s will. In the same way—and in fact the two are connected and of one consistent line of thought—the American *Declaration of Independence* goes on to say: “To secure these rights, governments are instituted among men, deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed; whenever any form of government becomes destructive of these ends, it is the right of the people to alter or abolish it. We hold these truths to be self-evident.”

⑦ God is just toward every individual; He treats all alike and with love. If an agent violates these “self-evident truths,” then the agent loses the qualification to act as agent. Thus, within the moral system of the organizational mode of association, the concept of rights arises. People must respect one another’s rights; the group, too, must safeguard these individual rights and prevent the agents of the group from abusing power. Hence the constitution appears. The idea of a constitution corresponds to the Western idea of public affairs. The state may demand service from the people, but the state must also guarantee that it will not violate the people’s rights, and that it will exercise power only within the bounds of justice and protection.

⑧ I have said quite a bit about the moral system in the “organizational mode of association” in order to set off more clearly the distinctive features of the moral system in the “differential mode of association.” Seen in terms of their differences, the two are in many respects exactly opposite. In a network of social relations centered on oneself, the most important principle is naturally “to subdue the self and return to ritual propriety,” and “in all things, self-cultivation is the root.” This is the starting point of the moral system in the differential mode of association.

⑨ The social sphere that is pushed outward from the self is made up of strand after strand of personal ties, and each strand is maintained by a particular moral element. The social sphere is extended outward from the “self,” and in this process of extension there are various routes. The most basic route is kinship: the relation between parent and child, and that among siblings. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect. “Filial piety and fraternal respect—are they not the root of humaneness?” Along another route are friends, for whom the corresponding virtues are loyalty and trustworthiness. “In planning on behalf of others, have I failed to be loyal? In associating with friends, have I failed to be trustworthy?” “Hold loyalty and trustworthiness as primary; have no friends who are not your equal.” Confucius once summarized this by saying: “A young man, at home, should be filial; outside, he should be respectful to his elders; he should be cautious and trustworthy, broadly love the multitude, and draw near to the humane.”
