① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “expanded family.” In these terms, the main word is “family,” and an adjective such as “small” or “large” is added to indicate the formal differences between Chinese and Western “families,” which are assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the standpoint of structural principle, the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia. What we ordinarily call the difference between a large family and a small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but a matter of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby qualify as a “large family,” while a Chia consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two cases are different.

③ In anthropology, the concept of the family has a clear definition: it is a procreative group consisting of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born to the married pair. This group is united for the birth and nurture of children. In any society in which the task of bearing and rearing children is undertaken by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, with respect to each individual family, is short-term. Once the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to conduct their own affairs of procreation and child-rearing, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not permanent in fact, yet they are not temporary, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the very beginning prepare for its own end. The purpose of bringing up children is precisely to bring the period of nurture to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ In every culture, however, the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains a group limited to parents and children, then both at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up there is a period in which it is merely a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Whatever requires the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are few; its chief function is the procreation and nurture of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential sequence of kinship relations. The members of what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which the structure expands is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilateral line: it includes only the paternal side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are outsiders. On the father’s side, however, expansion can extend very far. A Chia of five generations living under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to this unilateral principle of kinship has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not exactly the same as what we ordinarily call a Tsu, for a Tsu is made up of many Chia; it is a social group composed of social groups. For this reason, I introduce here the term “small lineage.” A small lineage and a large lineage are the same in structural principle; their difference lies in quantity, in size. This is why I prefer not to use “large family,” but instead use “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be identical with a family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relationship, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in a social circle. We cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can we say that it forms an independent unit by itself. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the procreative function, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, a clan is an organization for carrying on collective undertakings; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted the differential mode of association, using the moral relations of kinship to organize social groups and carry on various undertakings, thus making the basic Chia clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that, in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group therefore cannot be temporary, as the Western family is. The Chia must be continuous; it must not split apart when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a lineage. A clan is by nature long-lasting, just like our Chia. To call this kind of group a small lineage also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary nature of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be enough, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large, exceeding what husband and wife can manage, then brothers, paternal uncles, and others may all be brought together in one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the variation in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is the patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family group, husband and wife form the main axis. Together they manage the affairs of procreation and child-rearing. Children play a secondary role in the group, and when they grow up they leave it. Political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife therefore become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force that holds the group together. The development of affection between the sexes makes the family a center from which people obtain comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous group for carrying on undertakings, its main axes lie between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; they are vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axes, is not temporary, both axes, under the demands of the undertaking, exclude ordinary sentiment. By “ordinary sentiment” I mean something opposed to discipline. No undertaking can be separated from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must speak of discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of a social group organized for the carrying on of undertakings.
