① Western society is rather like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, several bundles into a larger bundle, and several larger bundles into a shoulder-load. Every single piece of firewood belongs, within the whole load, to a definite bundle, sub-bundle, and sheaf. Each piece can also be identified with the other pieces in the same sheaf, the same sub-bundle, or the same bundle; the divisions are clear and not confused. In society, such units are groups. When I say that Western social organization resembles bundled firewood, I mean to point out that it is often composed of one group after another, each formed by a number of people. A group has definite boundaries: who belongs inside the group and who stands outside it cannot be left ambiguous; the distinction must be clear. Those within the group constitute one body, and their relation to the group is the same. If there are subdivisions or ranks within the same group, these too are stipulated in advance. The analogy of bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may belong to several groups, whereas it is of course impossible for the same piece of firewood to be found in several bundles. This is where human beings differ from firewood. I use this metaphor merely to make more concrete a particular pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call it the **group mode of association**.

② In the West, the family is a group with clearly defined boundaries. If a friend writes to say that he will “bring his family” to visit you, he knows quite precisely which people will be coming with him. In China, by contrast, such a statement is highly ambiguous. In Britain and America, “family” includes the man himself, his wife, and their minor children. If he were coming only with his wife, he would not use the word “family.” In China, although the phrase “may your whole household honor us with your presence” is often heard, few people can say exactly whom this “household” is supposed to include.

③ This brings us to our own usage of words. The Chinese word **jia**—“family” or “household”—may be said to be the most elastic of all. “The person at home” may refer only to one’s wife; “family clan” may include a large number of uncles, cousins, and nephews; “one of our own people” may embrace anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s circle in order to express intimacy. The scope of “our own people” expands or contracts according to time and circumstance. It may become so extensive as to be beyond counting; truly, all under Heaven may become one family.

④ Why should the term for our most basic social unit be so vague and indeterminate? In my view, this indicates that the very structure of our society differs from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood tied up clearly one by one, but rather like the ripples that spread outward in circles when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water. Each person is the center of the circles produced by his or her social influence. Those reached by the ripples of these circles come into connection with that person. The circles that a person activates at a given time and place are not necessarily the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society have precisely this character of concentric ripples formed by a stone cast into water. Kinship relations are social relations generated on the basis of the facts of birth and marriage. The network formed through birth and marriage can be extended outward without limit, including people of the past, the present, and the future. The popular saying, “A cousinship may extend for three thousand li,” expresses this idea; indeed, “three thousand li” merely indicates the breadth of its possible reach. This network is like a spider’s web: it has a center, namely the self. Each of us possesses such a web extending outward through kinship relations, but no two webs cover exactly the same people. People in a society may use the same system to identify their relatives; what they share is only this system. The system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts. When we use this system to identify concrete relatives, however, the persons identified differ from one individual to another. Within the kinship system, we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized relatives are completely identical. Two brothers, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Thus, with respect to the network of social relations connected through kinship, the network is individualized. Each network has an “I” at its center, and the center of each network is different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of territorial relations. The modern **baojia** system is organized according to the group mode of association, but this is incompatible with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each household takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around itself; this circle is its “neighborhood.” When there is a wedding or other happy occasion, neighbors are invited to drink wine; when a child is born, red eggs are distributed; when there is a death, neighbors come forward to help prepare the body for burial and carry the coffin. It is an institution of mutual aid in daily life. Yet it is not a fixed group, but a range. The size of this range depends on the strength and influence of the center. The neighborhood of a powerful household may extend throughout the entire village, while that of a poor household may include only two or three adjacent families. This is much like our circles of kinship. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, for instance, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin on the paternal-aunt side, and Xue Baochai, a cousin on the maternal-aunt side; later still more people were added—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. So long as some kinship connection could be drawn, they could all be included. But once power and influence change, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle contracts into a small cluster. In extreme cases, it may be like Su Qin returning home in destitution: “his wife did not regard him as a husband, and his sister-in-law did not regard him as kin.” The differential mode of association in China’s traditional structure possesses precisely this capacity for expansion and contraction. In the countryside, a family may be very small; yet among wealthy landlords and bureaucratic strata, it may become so large as to resemble a small state. Chinese people are especially sensitive to the fickleness of human relationships precisely because these highly elastic social circles expand and contract with changes in the power and influence of their centers.

⑦ In Western society, where even grown children living at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, everyone recognizes the boundaries of the group. Those inside the group possess definite qualifications for membership. Once those qualifications are lost, one must leave the group. For them, this is not a matter of the warmth or coldness of human feeling, but a matter of rights. In Western society, people contend over rights; in ours, they seek connections and speak of personal ties.
