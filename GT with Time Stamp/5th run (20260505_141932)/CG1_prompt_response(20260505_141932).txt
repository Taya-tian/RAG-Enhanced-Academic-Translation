**Group One: Chinese and Western Social Relations (Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”)**

① Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a shoulder-load. Every single piece of wood in the whole load belongs to a definite bundle, sheaf, and handful. Each piece can also identify the other pieces in the same handful, the same sheaf, and the same bundle; the divisions are clear and do not become confused. In society, these units are groups. When I say that Western social organization resembles bundled firewood, I mean to indicate that it is often composed of one group after another, each made up of a number of people. A group has definite boundaries: who belongs inside the group and who is outside it cannot be left ambiguous; the distinction must be clear. Those within the group are of one body, and their relation to the group is the same. If there are subdivisions or ranks within the same group, these too are stipulated in advance. My analogy of bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may participate in several groups, whereas it is of course impossible for one stick of firewood to be found in several sheaves. This is where people differ from firewood. I use this metaphor only in order to make more concrete a certain pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call it the **group mode of association**.

② In the West, the family is a group with clearly defined boundaries. If a friend writes to say that he will “bring his family” to visit you, he knows quite well which people will be coming with him. In China, by contrast, such a statement is highly ambiguous. In Britain and America, the family includes the man himself, his wife, and their minor children. If he comes only with his wife, he would not say that he is bringing his “family.” In China, although the polite phrase “May your entire household honor us with your presence” is commonly seen, very few people can say exactly whom this “household” ought to include.

③ Speaking of our terminology, the word *jia*—“family” or “household”—is perhaps the most elastic of all. “The one at home” may refer only to one’s wife; “members of the family line” may refer to a large group including paternal uncles, cousins, and nephews; “our own people” may encompass anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s circle in order to express intimacy. The scope of “our own people” expands and contracts according to time and place; it can become so large as to be beyond counting—indeed, the whole world may become one family.

④ Why should the term for our most basic social unit be so indistinct? In my view, this reflects the fact that our social structure itself differs from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood tied clearly one by one; rather, it is like the ripples that spread outward in circles when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water. Each person is the center of the circles generated by his or her social influence. Whoever is reached by the ripples of these circles enters into connection with that person. The circles a person mobilizes at a given time and place are not necessarily the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society possess precisely this quality of concentric ripples formed by throwing a stone into water. Kinship relations are social relations arising from the facts of birth and marriage. The network formed through birth and marriage can be extended outward without limit to include countless people—past, present, and future. The common saying “a cousinship may extend three thousand *li*” expresses just this idea; the “three thousand *li*” merely indicates vast extent. This network is like a spider’s web, with a center—namely, oneself. Each of us has such a web extending outward through kinship relations, yet no two webs cover the same set of people. People in the same society may use the same system to identify their kin; what they share is only this system. The system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts concerning relatedness. When we use this system to identify concrete relatives, however, the persons identified by each individual are different. Within the kinship system, we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. Going a step further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized kin can be completely identical. Two brothers may indeed have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Therefore, insofar as the network of social relations formed through kinship is concerned, it is individual and particular. Each network has an “I” at its center, and the center of each network is different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of territorial relations. The modern *baojia* system is organized according to the group mode of association, but this is quite incompatible with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each household takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around itself; this circle is the “neighborhood.” When there is a celebration, neighbors are invited to a feast; when a child is born, red eggs are distributed; when there is a funeral, they come to help with encoffining and carrying the coffin. It is an institution of mutual aid in daily life. Yet it is not a fixed group, but a range or sphere. The size of this sphere depends on the strength or weakness of the center’s influence. The neighborhood of a powerful household may extend throughout the whole village, while that of a poor household may consist only of two or three adjacent families. This is the same as our circles of kinship. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, for instance, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin through a paternal aunt, and Xue Baochai, a cousin through a maternal aunt; later still more relatives appeared—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. Anyone who could be linked by kinship could be accommodated. But once power changes, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrinks into a small cluster. In extreme cases, it may resemble Su Qin’s impoverished return home, when “his wife did not regard him as her husband, nor his sister-in-law as her husband’s younger brother.” The differential mode of association in China’s traditional structure possesses precisely this capacity for expansion and contraction. In the countryside, a household may be very small; yet among wealthy landlords and the bureaucratic class, it may grow so large as to resemble a small state. The Chinese are especially sensitive to the fickleness of human relations precisely because these highly elastic social circles expand or contract with changes in the power of their center.

⑦ In Western society, where children who have reached adulthood and continue to live at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, people recognize the boundaries of the group. Membership in the group entails definite qualifications. Once those qualifications are lost, one must leave the group. For them, this is not a matter of warmth or coldness in human feeling, but a matter of rights. In Western society, people contend over rights; in ours, people seek connections and appeal to personal sentiment.
