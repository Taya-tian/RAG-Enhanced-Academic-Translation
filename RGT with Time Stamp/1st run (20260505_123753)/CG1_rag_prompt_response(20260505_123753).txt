Group One: Social Relations in China and the West  
(Chapter Four, “The Differential Mode of Association”)

① Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a load. Every single stalk of firewood belongs, within the whole load, to a definite bundle, sheaf, and handful. Each stalk can also find the other stalks in the same handful, the same sheaf, and the same bundle; the divisions are clear and cannot be confused. In society, these units are groups. When I say that Western social organization resembles bundled firewood, I mean to point out that it is often made up of separate groups formed by a number of people. A group has definite boundaries: who is inside the group and who is outside it cannot be left vague; the distinction must be clear. Those within the group are one body, and their relation to the group is the same. If there are subdivisions or ranks within the same group, these too are prescribed in advance. My analogy of bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may belong to several groups, whereas it is of course impossible for the same stalk of firewood to be in several sheaves at once. This is where human beings differ from firewood. I use this metaphor in order to make more concrete a certain pattern of relationships among people in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

② In the West, the family is a group with clearly defined boundaries. If a friend writes to say that he is going to “bring his family” to visit you, he knows very well who the people coming with him will be. In China, such a phrase is very ambiguous. In Britain and America, a family includes the man himself, his wife, and his minor children. If he were coming only with his wife, he would not use the word “family.” In China, although the polite expression “may your whole household honor us with your presence” is often heard, very few people could say exactly whom this “household” is supposed to include.

③ Speaking of our terms, the word jia, “family” or “home,” is perhaps the most flexible of all. “The one at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “members of the family line” may refer to a large number of uncles, cousins, and nephews; “our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle in order to show intimacy. The range of “our own people” expands and contracts according to time and place. It can grow so large as to be beyond counting—indeed, all under Heaven may become one family.

④ Why should the name of our most basic social unit be so vague and indistinct? In my view, this reveals that our social structure itself is different from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood tied up clearly one by one; rather, it is like the circles of ripples that spread out when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water. Each person is the center of the circles produced by his social influence. Whoever is reached by the ripples of these circles enters into a relationship with him. The circles a person makes use of at a given time and place are not necessarily the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society have precisely this character of concentric ripples formed by throwing a stone into water. Kinship is a social relationship arising from the facts of birth and marriage. The network formed through birth and marriage can be extended outward without limit to include an infinite number of people—past, present, and future. Our common saying, “a maternal cousin three thousand li away,” expresses just this idea; the “three thousand li” is merely a way of indicating great extension. This network is like a spider’s web, with a center, namely oneself. Each one of us has such a web cast outward through kinship relations, but no two webs cover the same people. People in a society may use the same system to identify their kin; what is the same is only this system. A system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts of relationship. When we use this system to identify concrete relatives, the people each person identifies are different. In the kinship system, all of us have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized kin can be exactly the same. Two brothers, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Therefore, as a network of social relations linked together by kinship, it is individual. Each network has the “self” as its center, and the centers of the various networks are all different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of territorial relations. The modern baojia system is patterned on the organizational mode of association, but it is incompatible with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each household takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around itself; this circle is its “neighborhood.” When there is a wedding or other happy occasion, neighbors are invited to a feast; when a child is born, red eggs are sent to them; when there is a death, they come out to help with the encoffining and to carry the coffin. It is an institution of mutual aid in daily life. But it is not a fixed group; it is a range. The size of this range depends on the strength and influence of the center. The neighborhood of a powerful household may extend over the whole village, while that of a poor household may include only the two or three adjacent families. This is the same as our kinship circles. In the Prospect Garden of the Jia family, for example, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin on the father’s sister’s side, and Xue Baochai, a cousin on the mother’s sister’s side; later there were even more, such as Baoqin and Xiuyan. So long as a kinship connection could be drawn, they could all be included. But once power and influence change, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle contracts into a small cluster. In extreme cases, it may be like Su Qin returning home in poverty: “his wife no longer regarded him as her husband, and his sister-in-law no longer regarded him as her husband’s younger brother.” The differential mode of association in China’s traditional structure has this capacity to expand and contract. In the countryside, the family may be very small; but among wealthy landlords and the bureaucratic class, it may become as large as a small state. Chinese people are especially sensitive to the warmth and coldness of human relationships precisely because these highly flexible social circles expand or shrink with changes in the power and influence of their centers.

⑦ In Western society, where even children who have grown up and still live at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, everyone recognizes the boundaries of the group. Those within the group possess definite qualifications. Once those qualifications are lost, one must leave the group. For them, this is not a matter of human feelings growing warm or cold, but a matter of rights. In Western society, people contend over rights; among us, people seek to pull connections and appeal to personal feelings.
