1. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I have called a “differential mode of association”: a “network made up of strand after strand of personal connections.” This pattern differs from the “organizational mode of association” of the modern West. In an organizational mode, relationships among individuals depend on a common framework: first there is this framework, and then each person becomes attached to it and thereby enters into relations with others. The idea of the “citizen” necessarily presupposes the prior existence of the “state.” This structure may well have been handed down from the “tribal” form of primitive peoples. In a nomadic economy, the tribal form very clearly exhibits an “organizational mode of association.” A group of people whose lives depend on one another cannot survive alone and scattered in the mountains and forests. For them, the “group” is the precondition of life. But in a settled rural society, when each person can live from the land by his own labor, the need for companions is felt only in accidental and temporary emergencies. For such people, relations with others arise later and are secondary; moreover, in different situations they require different degrees of association, and do not have a pronounced need for a constant and widely extended group. Thus their society adopts a “differential mode of association.”

2. Differences in the pattern of social structure give rise to different moral ideas. Moral ideas are the beliefs, held consciously by people living in society, that they ought to observe the norms of social conduct. They include norms of conduct, the convictions of the actors, and social sanctions. Their content consists of the norms governing relations between person and person, and is determined by the pattern of that society. From the social point of view, morality is the sanctioning force exerted by society upon individual conduct, making people act in accordance with prescribed forms so as to maintain the survival and continuity of that society.

3. In an “organizational mode of association,” the basic moral ideas are built upon the relationship between the group and the individual. The group is a “reality” that transcends the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot point to some concrete, physical object and say: this is the group. It is a bundle of relationships among people; it is a force controlling the behavior of each individual; it is an object upon which the lives of its constituent members depend; it is a common will that exists before any individual and yet cannot be separated from individuals. Such a “reality” can only be symbolized and represented by tangible things. Only in societies with an “organizational mode of association” does the idea of an all-encompassing God arise. The relationship of the group to the individual is symbolized in the relationship of God to the believer: God is a judge who rewards and punishes, an impartial maintainer of order, an omnipotent protector.

4. If we wish to understand the moral system of Western society in its “organizational mode of association,” we cannot separate it from Western religious ideas. Religious piety and trust are not only the source of their moral ideas; they are also the force that supports norms of conduct and the symbol of the group. Under the idea of God as the symbol of the group, there are two important derivative ideas: first, that every individual is equal before God; second, that God is just toward every individual.

5. Jesus called God the Father—a father common to every person—and he even denied, in front of the crowd, the parents who had given birth to him. In order to carry through this idea of “equality,” in Christian mythology Jesus was born of a virgin. Here the particular and private bond between parent and child is denied. In fact, this is not “nonsense,” but a powerful symbol: it symbolizes that the “commonly held” group, and the representative of the group—God—must be without private interest. Every “son of man,” every “constituent member of the group” symbolized by Jesus, must, beyond his privately possessed father, have a more important father shared with others: the “Heavenly Father,” that is, the group. Only in this way can equality in each person’s moral personality be established; the relationship between each member of the group and the group is equal. The group cannot be privately possessed by any individual. On this basis arises the opening statement of the American Declaration of Independence: “All men are born equal; they have Heaven-endowed, inalienable rights.”

6. Yet God is in the unseen realm, which precisely symbolizes the invisible reality of the group. But when the will of the group is to be carried out, there must still be people who act on its behalf. The “agent,” or *minister*, is a basic concept in societies with an organizational mode of association. The pastor who carries out God’s will is a *minister*; the official who carries out the power of the group is also a *minister*. Both are “agents,” not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and the pastor, between the state and the government, must not be confused. In the history of Christianity, people have again and again demanded direct communication with God, rebelling against the “agents” who could not truly represent God’s will. In the same way—indeed, the two are connected in practice and may be said to be consistent—the American Declaration of Independence goes on to say: “In order to secure these rights, governments are instituted among men, deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed; if any form of government becomes destructive of these ends, the people have the right to alter or abolish it. We hold these truths to be self-evident.”

7. God is just toward every individual; he treats all alike; he is loving. If an agent violates these “self-evident truths,” then the agent loses the qualification to act as agent. Thus, within the moral system of the organizational mode of association, the idea of rights arises. People must mutually respect one another’s rights, and the group must also guarantee these individual rights and prevent the agents of the group from abusing power. Thus the constitution comes into being. The constitutional idea is linked with the Western idea of public office. The state may demand service from the people, but the state must also guarantee that it will not infringe upon the people’s rights, and that it will exercise power within the bounds of justice and care.

8. I have said a great deal about the moral system in the “organizational mode of association” in order to set off the special characteristics of the moral system in the “differential mode of association.” Viewed in terms of their differences, the two are in many respects precisely opposite. In a network of social relations centered on the self, the most important principle is naturally “restraining the self and returning to ritual propriety,” and “in all things taking self-cultivation as the root.” This is the starting point of the moral system in the differential mode of association.

9. The social sphere extended outward from the self is made up of strand after strand of personal connections, and each strand is maintained by a particular moral element. The social sphere is pushed outward from the “self,” and in this process of extension there are various routes. The most basic is kinship: the relations between parent and child and between siblings. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect. “Filial piety and fraternal respect—are they not the root of humanity?” Extending along another route, there are friends; the corresponding elements are loyalty and trustworthiness. “In planning on behalf of others, have I failed to be loyal? In dealings with friends, have I failed to be trustworthy?” “Make loyalty and trustworthiness your main principles; have no friends who are not as good as yourself.” Confucius once summed this up by saying: “A young man, at home, should be filial; abroad, he should be respectful to his elders; he should be cautious and trustworthy, love the multitude broadly, and draw close to the humane.”
