4. The idea of the “citizen” necessarily presupposes the prior existence of the “state.”

5. A group of people whose lives depend upon one another cannot survive alone and scattered in the mountains and forests.

6. The idea of constitutional government goes together with the Western idea of public affairs.

7. This community is united for the purpose of the birth and rearing of children.

8. On the father’s side, however, it can be extended very far. A family of five generations living under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within those five generations.

9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force that holds the group together.

10. In my book *The American Character*, I used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. “The one at home” may refer only to one’s own wife; “members of the family gate” may refer to a large number of uncles and nephews; “one of our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle as a sign of intimacy. The range of “our own people” can expand or contract according to time and place; it may become so large as to be beyond counting—indeed, the whole world may become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I call a “differential mode of association”: a network made up of line after line of personal relationships. This pattern is different from the “group mode of association” found in the modern West.

13. But as I see it now, that term is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group in Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

14. Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be identical with the family. For the basis of the kinship structure is the parent-child relationship—the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, a clan is a corporate organization; if further enlarged, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thus giving the basic family a clan-like character.

17. The Chinese family is a corporate organization; its size is determined by the scale of the undertakings it carries on.

18. Since our family is a continuing corporate community, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. It is vertical, not horizontal.

19. In using this metaphor, I am trying to make concrete a certain pattern of human relationships in social life. We may call it the group mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system belongs to the group mode of association, but this is entirely incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward without limit, including an infinite number of people—past, present, and future. The common saying, “a cousin relationship extends three thousand li,” expresses precisely this idea; in fact, “three thousand li” merely indicates the breadth of such extension.

22. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized kin are exactly the same. Two brothers may, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is much like our kinship circle. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin through a paternal aunt, and Xue Baochai, a cousin through a maternal aunt; later still there were Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. So long as some kinship tie could be claimed, they could all be included. But once the balance of power changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrank into a small group.

24. Taking the self as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relationships formed with others are not like members of a group all standing on the same plane. They are like ripples on the surface of water, spreading outward circle by circle; the farther they extend, the more attenuated they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The ten *lun* discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—between spirits and men, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, intimate and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, political affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means properly distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order of graded distinctions.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “A man who is filial and fraternal yet fond of offending his superiors is rare; one who is not fond of offending his superiors yet fond of rebellion has never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a single continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the five relationships as “the universal Way of all under Heaven.”

27. A group is a “reality” that transcends the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot point to some concrete object with physical form and say, “This is the group.” It is a bundle of human relationships, a force that controls the behavior of each individual, an object upon which the lives of its members depend, a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot exist apart from individuals.

28. The “minister,” or agent, is a basic concept in a society organized according to the group mode of association. The priest who carries out the will of God is a minister; the official who carries out the authority of the group is also a minister. Both are agents; they are not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and priest, and between state and government, must not be confused.

29. The social sphere extended outward from the self is made up of line after line of personal connection, each line held together by a moral element. The social sphere is pushed outward from the “self,” and in this process of extension there are various routes. The most basic is kinship: parent-child relations and relations among siblings. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect.

30. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. This community is united for the birth and rearing of children. In societies where individuals bear the responsibility for producing and rearing children, such a community is indispensable.
