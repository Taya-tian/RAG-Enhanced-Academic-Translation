**Group Two: Chinese and Western Egocentrism and Individualism  
(Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”)**

① Taking the “self” as the center, one is like a stone thrown into water: the social relationships formed with others are not like the members of a group all standing on the same plane, but rather like ripples on water, spreading outward circle by circle—the farther they extend, the more attenuated they become. Here we encounter a basic characteristic of Chinese social structure. What Confucianism has examined most carefully is *human relationships* (*lun*). What is *lun*? My interpretation is that it refers to the differentiated sequence of ripples that arise among those people who enter into social relations with oneself as one extends outward from the self. The *Shiming* also says under the character *lun* 沦: “*Lun* means order; the patterns of water follow one another and thus possess *lunli*.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once observed that all meanings sharing the component *lun* 仑 as a common denominator are interconnected: “What they jointly express is a sense of pattern, category, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* emphasizes differentiation. The “ten *lun*” discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—spirits and gods, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and humble, close and distant, ranks and rewards, husband and wife, affairs of government, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to distinctions of degree. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order of graded differences. To us today, how can concrete social relations such as spirits and gods, ruler and minister, father and son, and husband and wife be placed on the same footing as abstract relative positions such as noble and humble, intimate and distant, near and far, superior and inferior? Yet in our traditional social structure, the most basic concept—the guiding order in the network constituted by interpersonal dealings—is precisely a differential sequence, that is, *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: “Loving one’s kin as kin, honoring the honorable, treating elders as elders, and distinguishing between men and women—these are things that cannot be altered among the people.” The meaning is that the framework of this social structure cannot be changed; what changes are only the things done by making use of this framework.

③ What Confucius stressed most was the word “extend” (*tui*)—the outward expansion of ripples on water. He first acknowledged a self, the self from which one extends to others. This self must be restrained by ritual propriety; subduing the self is self-cultivation. Following this concentric order of human relations, one can then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “Those who are filial and fraternal as persons, yet fond of offending their superiors, are few indeed; and those who are not fond of offending superiors yet fond of creating disorder—such people have never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven, there is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the five relationships as “the universal Way under Heaven.” Because in this type of social structure one extends outward circle by circle from the self to all under Heaven, Mencius said that he was simply “good at extending.”

④ Within this highly elastic network, there is always, at every time and place, a “self” serving as the center. This is not individualism, but egocentrism. The individual is defined in relation to the group; it is the part in relation to the whole. Under individualism, on the one hand, there is the notion of equality, meaning that the positions of all members within the same group are equal and that the individual may not infringe upon the rights of all. On the other hand, there is the notion of constitutionalism, meaning that the group cannot efface the individual and may exercise control over the individual only with respect to those rights that the individual is willing to surrender. These notions must first presuppose the existence of the group. In traditional Chinese thought, we do not have this set of assumptions, because what we have is egocentrism: a doctrine in which all values take the “self” as their center.

⑤ Egocentrism is not limited to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair even if it benefited the whole world; the Confucians should also be included. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu neglected the relative and elastic character of egocentrism. He was too rigid-minded, clinging obstinately to the self and refusing to let go. Confucius, by contrast, knew how to extend from the self to others; yet even when this extension reached to the four seas, the center still remained the self. The Master said: “To govern by virtue is like the North Star: it dwells in its place, and the multitude of stars revolve around it.” This is an excellent metaphor for the differential mode of association. The self is always the center, like the North Star, unmoving through the four seasons, while all others revolve around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus had a collective entity transcending the individual: he had his Kingdom of Heaven, and therefore he could sacrifice himself in order to fulfill the Kingdom of Heaven. Confucius was not so.

⑥ Zigong said: “If there were someone who broadly bestowed benefits upon the people and was able to aid the multitude, what would you say of him? Could he be called humane?” The Master said: “Why merely humane? He would surely have to be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would have found this difficult. As for the humane person: wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to achieve for himself, he helps others achieve. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy—this may be called the method of humaneness.”

⑦ In Confucius’s moral system, he never departs from the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks it in himself; the petty person seeks it in others.” For this reason, he could not, like Jesus, love all under Heaven universally—even to the point of loving his enemies and praying to God to forgive those who killed him. Such acts do not proceed from a self-centered point of departure. What of Confucius? Someone asked: “What do you think of repaying resentment with virtue?” The Master said: “Then with what would one repay virtue? Repay resentment with uprightness, and repay virtue with virtue.” This is a hierarchy of differential gradations, and Confucius never relaxes it. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, use the narrow self to respond to every situation; rather, he expanded or contracted the moral sphere according to need. He was not like Jesus, or like China’s Mo Di, for whom once the extension was released, it could not be drawn back.
