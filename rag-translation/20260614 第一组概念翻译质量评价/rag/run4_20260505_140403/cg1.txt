① Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a carrying load. Every single stalk of wood belongs, within the whole load, to a definite bundle, sheaf, and handful. Each stalk can also find the other stalks in the same handful, the same sheaf, and the same bundle; the divisions are clear and do not become confused. In society, these units are groups. When I say that Western social organization resembles bundles of firewood, I mean to indicate that people there are often organized into one group after another. A group has definite boundaries: who is inside the group and who is outside it cannot be left vague; the distinction must be clear. Those within the group form one body, and their relation to the group is the same. If there are subdivisions or ranks within the same group, these too are stipulated in advance. My comparison with bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may belong to several groups, whereas it is of course impossible for the same stalk of wood to be in several sheaves. This is where human beings differ from firewood. I use this metaphor only in the hope of making more concrete a certain pattern of relations among people in social life. We may call it the group pattern of association.

② In the West, the family is a group with clearly defined boundaries. If a friend writes to you saying that he is going to “bring his family” to visit you, he knows perfectly well which people will be coming with him. In China, this expression is extremely vague. In England and America, a family includes a man, his wife, and their minor children. If he came only with his wife, he would not use the word “family.” In China, although the phrase “the whole household honors us with its presence” is often seen, very few people can say exactly whom this “household” ought to include.

③ Since we have mentioned our use of words, the Chinese word *jia* may be said to be the most elastic of all. “Those at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “the family gate” may refer to a large number of uncles, cousins, and nephews; “our own people” may include anyone one wishes to draw into one’s own circle in order to show intimacy. The range of “our own people” can expand or contract according to time and place. It can become so large as to be beyond counting—truly, “all under Heaven can become one family.”

④ Why is it that the term for our most basic social unit should be so unclear and imprecise? In my view, this shows that our social structure itself is different from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood tied up clearly one by one; it is rather like the circles of ripples spreading outward when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water. Each person is the center of the circles pushed outward by his social influence. Whoever is reached by the ripples of these circles enters into relation with him. The circles a person makes use of at a given time and place are not necessarily the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society have precisely this character of concentric ripples formed by throwing a stone into water. Kinship relations are social relations arising from the facts of birth and marriage. The network formed through birth and marriage can be extended outward without limit to include countless people—past, present, and future. Our common saying, “one maternal cousinship stretches three thousand *li*,” expresses just this idea; in fact, “three thousand *li*” merely indicates great breadth. This network is like a spider’s web: it has a center, namely oneself. Each of us has such a web spread out through kinship relations, but no two webs cover the same people. People in a society may use the same system to identify their relatives; what they share is only this system. The system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts concerning relations. But when we use this system to identify concrete relatives, the people each of us identifies are different. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized relatives are completely identical. Two brothers may indeed have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Thus, as a network of social relations linked together by kinship, it is individual. Every network has an “I” as its center, and the centers of the various networks are all different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of territorial relations. The modern *baojia* system has the character of the group pattern, but this is quite incompatible with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each family takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around it; this circle is the “neighborhood.” When there is a wedding or other happy occasion, people are invited to drink wine; when a child is born, red eggs are sent; when there is a death, people come out to help with the encoffining and to carry the coffin. It is an institution of mutual aid in daily life. But it is not a fixed group; it is a range. The size of this range depends on the strength or weakness of the influence of the center. The neighborhood of a powerful family may extend over the whole village, while that of a poor family may include only two or three adjacent households. This is just like our circle of kinship. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, for example, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin on the paternal aunt’s side, and Xue Baochai, a cousin on the maternal aunt’s side; later there were still more—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. Anyone who could be connected as kin could be included. But when power changes, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrinks into a small cluster. In the extreme case, it can be like Su Qin returning home in poverty: “his wife did not regard him as a husband, and his sister-in-law did not regard him as an uncle.” The differential mode of association in China’s traditional structure possesses this capacity for expansion and contraction. In the countryside, a family may be very small; but among wealthy landlords and the bureaucratic class, it may become as large as a small state. Chinese people are especially sensitive to the warmth and coldness of human relations precisely because these highly elastic social circles expand or contract with changes in the power of the center.

⑦ In Western society, where even grown children living at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, everyone recognizes the boundaries of the group. Those inside the group have definite qualifications. Once those qualifications are lost, one must leave the group. For them, this is not a matter of warmth or coldness in human feeling, but a matter of rights. In Western society people struggle over rights; in ours, they seek connections and speak of personal feelings.
