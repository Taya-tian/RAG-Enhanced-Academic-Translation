**Group Four: Chinese and Western Chia (Chapter VI, “The Lineage-Family”)**

① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, a group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “expanded family.” In all these terms the substantive word is “family”; adjectives such as “small” or “large” are added to it in order to indicate the formal difference between Chinese and Western “families,” which are assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite proper. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage-family.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the difference between Chinese and Western Chia from the standpoint of structural principle. The difference between what we ordinarily call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A Chia with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions of a “large family,” while a Chia consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two are different.

③ The concept of family has a definite definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married couple. This group is formed for the birth and nurture of children. In any society where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a group cannot be absent. But the procreative function, so far as each individual family is concerned, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the nurture of their parents and go on to manage the procreation and nurture of children of their own, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Social groups such as schools and states are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must prepare for its own conclusion from the moment it begins. The purpose of bringing up children lies precisely in bringing that nurture to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in any culture the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The co-operation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains confined to the group constituted by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is simply the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife may indeed carry on economic, emotional, and sexual co-operation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Any undertaking requiring the co-operation of a larger number of persons must be carried on by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are few, chiefly the procreation and nurture of children. But in Chinese rural society the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, extend outward along the differential order of kinship. Those who constitute what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which the structure expands is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands unilaterally—that is, it includes only the father’s side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law at the same time. Under the patrilineal principle, the son-in-law and the married daughter are both members of an outside Chia. On the father’s side, however, expansion may go very far. A Chia in which five generations live under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to this unilateral kinship principle has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. In structure, our Chia is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a Tsu, for what we call a Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group of social groups. For this reason I propose here the term “small lineage-family.” The small lineage-family and the large lineage-family are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity or size. This is why I am unwilling to use “large family” and instead use “small lineage-family.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ In structure, the lineage-family includes the family; the smallest lineage-family may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage-family is extended from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage-family is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit by itself. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Although the lineage-family includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on undertakings; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex functions—political, economic, religious, and so on. Our Chia is precisely like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be entrusted to the lineage-family; on the other hand, we may also say that, in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children, but must be expanded. Moreover, all political, economic, religious, and similar affairs require long-term continuity. This basic social group cannot, like the Western family, be temporary. The Chia must be continuous; it must not divide because an individual has grown up, nor end because an individual has died. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a clan. A clan is by nature enduring, just like our Chia. To call this kind of group a small lineage-family also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on undertakings, and its size is determined by the size of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the co-operation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, brothers and paternal uncles may all gather into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to assume many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly carry on the procreative undertaking; children are secondary figures in this group, and when they have grown up they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the cohesive force. The development of sexual and emotional attachment makes the family a center from which comfort in life is obtained. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous undertaking group, its main axes are between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; they are vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are nevertheless compelled by the needs of undertaking to exclude ordinary feelings. By “ordinary feelings” I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private sentiment. In the Chinese family there is family discipline; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are stressed. All these are characteristics of a group organized for carrying on undertakings.
