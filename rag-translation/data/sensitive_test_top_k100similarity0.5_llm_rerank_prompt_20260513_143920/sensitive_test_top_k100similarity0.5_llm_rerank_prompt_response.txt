4. The concept of the “citizen” necessarily presupposes the existence of a “state.”

5. A group of people whose lives are interdependent cannot seek survival singly and scattered in the mountains and forests.

6. The constitutional concept is matched with the Western concept of public affairs.

7. The association of this social group is for the birth and upbringing of children.

8. On the father’s side, however, it can be extended very far. A Chia of five generations living under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes is the cohesive force.

10. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. “Those at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “members of the family line” may refer to a large number of uncles and nephews; “our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle in order to show intimacy. The range of “our own people” is elastic according to time and place; it may become so large as to be beyond counting—indeed, all under Heaven may become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I have called a “differential mode of association,” that is, “a network formed by strands of personal connections.” This pattern is different from the modern Western “organizational mode of association.”

13. But as I now see it, this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group in Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

14. In structure, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the basis of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, a clan is a corporate organization, and when further expanded it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to combine social groups and carry on various undertakings, thus making the basic Chia clan-like in character.

17. The Chinese Chia is a corporate organization; its size is determined by the size of the undertakings it carries on.

18. Since our Chia is a continuous corporate social group, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal.

19. I use this metaphor in order to make more concrete a certain pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system belongs to the organizational mode of association, but it is incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended indefinitely to include an infinite number of persons—past, present, and future. The common saying, “one maternal cousinship reaches three thousand li,” expresses this idea; the “three thousand li” merely indicates the breadth of its possible extension.

22. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two persons under Heaven who can recognize exactly the same set of relatives. Two brothers, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is generally true of our kinship circle. In the Grand View Garden of the Jia family, for instance, there could live the paternal cousin Lin Daiyu, the maternal cousin Xue Baochai, and later still others such as Baoqin and Xiuyan; anyone who could be connected as a relative could be accommodated. But once the balance of power changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle contracted into a small group.

24. Taking the self as the centre, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like members of a group all standing on the same plane; rather, they are like ripples on water, spreading outward circle by circle—the farther they extend, the more distant and thinner they become.

25. Lun emphasizes distinctions. The ten lun discussed in the “Ji Tong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and humble, intimate and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, government affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded distinctions. “Not losing one’s lun” means distinguishing father from son, near from far, intimate from distant. Lun is an order of differences.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “Those who are filial and fraternal yet fond of offending their superiors are few; those who are not fond of offending their superiors yet fond of creating disorder have never existed.” From the self to the Chia, from the Chia to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is one continuous path. In the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the five lun are taken as “the universal Way under Heaven.”

27. A group is a “reality” transcending the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot concretely produce some physical object and say, “This is the group.” It is a bundle of human relations, a force controlling the behaviour of individuals, an object upon which the lives of its component members depend, a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot exist apart from individuals.

28. “Minister,” or “agent,” is a fundamental concept in a society organized according to the organizational mode of association. The pastor who carries out the will of God is a Minister; the official who carries out the power of the group is also a Minister. Both are “agents,” not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and the pastor, and between the state and the government, cannot be confused.

29. The social sphere constructed by extension outward from the self consists of strands of personal connections, each strand being maintained by a moral element. The social sphere is pushed outward from the “self,” and in this process of extension there are various routes. The most fundamental is kinship: parent-child relations and relations among siblings. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect.

30. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. The association of this social group is for the birth and upbringing of children. In a society where individuals bear the responsibility of procreation and child-rearing, this kind of social group will not be lacking.
