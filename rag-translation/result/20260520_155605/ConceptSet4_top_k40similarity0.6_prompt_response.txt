**Group Four: Chinese and Western Chia**  
**Chapter Six, “Lineage”**

① What I should like to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, a group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “expanded family.” The principal term in these expressions is “family,” and adjectives such as “small” or “large” are added to indicate formal differences between Chinese and Western “families” which are assumed to be essentially of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be “small lineage.”

② My reason for introducing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the principle of structure, the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia. The difference between what we commonly call the large family and the small family is certainly not a matter of size, not a matter of the number of persons included in the group, but a matter of structure. A family with more than ten children does not for that reason constitute a “large family,” while a family consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles used in the two cases are different.

③ The concept of the family has a definite meaning in anthropology: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relationship is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married pair. This group is united for the bearing and rearing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is undertaken by individuals, such a group is indispensable. But the procreative function, in the case of each individual family, is a short-term one. When the children grow up, they leave the nurture of their parents and go off to conduct their own affairs of bearing and raising children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect the family is not quite like ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary groups. Although in fact they are not eternal, they are nevertheless not provisional, for the functions they perform are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the very beginning prepare for its own end. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to a group composed of parents and children, then both at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up there is a period when it consists merely of the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, engage in economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can conduct are greatly limited. All affairs requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are also few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. The members of this group may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members constituting what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line along which it expands is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line—that is, it includes only the father’s side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law at the same time. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of another family. On the father’s side, however, expansion may go very far. A Chia with five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ In anthropology, a social group organized according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a special name: clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not quite the same as what we ordinarily call a tsu, for what we call a tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I have proposed here the term “small lineage.” A small lineage and a large lineage are the same in structural principle; their difference lies in number, in size. This is why I am unwilling to use “large family,” and use instead “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family. The smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the foundation of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, a clan is an organization for undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical order of kinship to organize social groups and conduct various undertakings, thus making the basic Chia clan-like in character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be undertaken through the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that, in order to conduct so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be enlarged. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group certainly cannot be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous; it cannot split apart when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a lineage. A clan is by nature long-term, just as our Chia is. To call this kind of social group a “small lineage” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast with the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of the undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient to handle it, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, then brothers, uncles, and nephews may all gather into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But regardless of how great the differences in size may be, the structural principle is consistent: it is a unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to assume many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the principal axis. Husband and wife jointly conduct the affairs of procreation; children play supporting roles in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Among them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are undertaken by other groups and do not fall within the province of the family. Husband and wife become the principal axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force that holds the group together. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous social group for undertakings, its principal axes are between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; they are vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the principal axis, is not temporary, both axes are compelled by the needs of undertakings to exclude ordinary affections. By “ordinary affections” I mean those contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can be separated from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feelings. In the Chinese Chia there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are stressed. These are all characteristics of a social group organized for undertakings.
