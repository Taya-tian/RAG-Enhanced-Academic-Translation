**Group Four: The Chinese and Western Chia (Chapter Six, “Chia-tsu”)**

① What I wish to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “Expanded Family.” In these terms the main word is “family”; an adjective such as “small” or “large” is added to indicate a formal difference between the Chinese and Western family, while assuming that they are of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite proper. A more exact term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be “small Chia-tsu.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the principle of structure, the difference between the Chia in Chinese and Western society. The difference between what we ordinarily call the large family and the small family is certainly not a difference of size, not a difference in the number of persons included in the group, but a difference in structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large family”; a Chia consisting only of four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles employed in the two cases are different.

③ The concept of family has a definite definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. Parents and children indicate its structure; procreation indicates its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married couple. This group is united for the bearing and rearing of children. In a society where the task of bearing and rearing children is assumed by individuals, such a group is indispensable. But the procreative function, for each particular family, is short-term. Once the children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to conduct their own affairs of bearing and rearing children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not entirely the same as ordinary social groups. Such groups as schools and states are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the beginning prepare for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children lies precisely in bringing rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in any culture, the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The co-operation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains a group limited to parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is merely the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual co-operation, but the affairs they conduct are greatly limited. All affairs requiring the co-operation of a larger number of persons must be carried on by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can conduct are few, the principal one being the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. The members of this group may be extended outward as need requires, along the differential sequence of kinship. The persons composing what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. But structurally the line of expansion is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilateral line, that is, it includes only the father’s side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of an outside Chia. On the father’s side, however, it may expand very far. A Chia of “five generations under one roof” may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to the principle of unilateral kinship has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a Tsu, because what we call a Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small Chia-tsu.” The small Chia-tsu and the large Chia-tsu are the same in structural principle; they differ in number, in size. This is why I do not wish to use “large family,” but instead use “small Chia-tsu.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the Chia-tsu includes the family. The smallest Chia-tsu may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the basis of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The Chia-tsu is derived from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the Chia-tsu is only one ring in the social circle. We cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can we say that it forms an independent unit by itself. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the Chia-tsu includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is just such a group. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to conduct various undertakings, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be assumed by the Chia-tsu. On the other hand, we may also say that, in order to conduct so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small grouping of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long continuity. This basic social group cannot be temporary in the way the Western family is. The Chia must be continuous; it must not divide because an individual has grown up, nor end because an individual has died. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of the Tsu. The clan is by nature long-term, just as our Chia is. To call this kind of social group a small Chia-tsu also indicates this long-term character, in contrast with the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for undertakings, and its size is determined by the size of the undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the co-operation of husband and wife may be sufficient to handle it, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, brothers and paternal uncles may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why, in our rural society, the size of the Chia can vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilateral, patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to assume many other functions, the content of the relations among the members of this group also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly conduct the affairs of procreation; the children play supporting roles in this group, and leave it when they grow up. For them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are assumed by other groups and do not fall within the province of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes becomes the cohesive force. The development of sexual affection makes their family the centre from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia is markedly different in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous group for undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son, between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes, because of the needs of the undertaking, exclude ordinary feelings. By ordinary feelings I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private affection. In the Chinese family there is family discipline; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are subject to the standards of the “Three Obediences and Four Virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of a group organized for undertakings.
