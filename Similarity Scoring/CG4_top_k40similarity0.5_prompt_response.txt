① What I wish to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” The principal word in these terms is “family,” and an adjective such as “small” or “large” is added to it in order to indicate the formal difference between the Chinese and Western “family,” assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the standpoint of structural principles, the difference between the Chia in Chinese and Western society. What we ordinarily call the difference between the large-family and the small family is by no means a difference in size, nor in the number of persons included in the group, but a difference in structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large-family,” while a household consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles on which the two are based are different.

③ The concept of family has a clear definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group consisting of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married couple. The union of this group is for the birth and rearing of children. In any society in which individuals assume the task of bearing and rearing children, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, so far as each individual family is concerned, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to undertake the business of bearing and rearing children of their own, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Groups such as schools and states are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must prepare for its own end from the very beginning. The purpose of rearing children is to bring the rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Procreation*.

④ Yet in any culture, the family group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The cooperation between husband and wife does not come to an end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to the group composed of parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and again after the children have grown up there is a period in which it is only a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Any affair requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be undertaken by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are also few; its chief function is the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundaries. The members of this group may expand outward according to need, along the differential order of kinship relations. The members who make up what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line along which it expands is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line—that is, it includes only the father’s side. Apart from a few exceptions, the Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law at the same time. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of another Chia. On the father’s side, however, it may expand very far. A Chia of five generations living under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ Such a social group, organized according to the principle of unilineal kinship, has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not quite the same as what we ordinarily call a tsu, for what we call a tsu is composed of many Chia and is a group of groups. For this reason I have introduced here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are the same in structural principle; they differ only in quantity, in size. This is why I am unwilling to use “large-family” and instead use “small lineage.” A difference of one character indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family. The smallest lineage may even be equal to a family. This is because the foundation of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is derived from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in a social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the procreative function, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on affairs; if further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted the differential mode of association, using the moral order of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on all sorts of affairs, thus causing the basic Chia to become clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions may all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that, in order to carry on these many affairs, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity, and this basic social group can by no means be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous. It must not divide when an individual grows up, nor end when an individual dies. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a lineage. The clan is originally long-term, just like our Chia. To call this kind of social group a small lineage also indicates this long-term quality, in contrast with the temporariness of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on affairs, and its size is determined by the size of the affairs to be managed. If the affairs are small, the cooperation of husband and wife is enough to handle them, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the affairs are large and exceed what husband and wife can bear, brothers, elder and younger paternal uncles, and others may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle is consistent: it is a unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly carry on the affairs of procreation, while children play a secondary role in this group; when they grow up, they leave it. For them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the family’s province. Since husband and wife become the main axis, the emotion between the sexes becomes the cohesive force. The development of sexual and emotional feeling makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for carrying on affairs, its main axes are between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. They are vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes exclude ordinary feelings because of the needs of the affairs to be carried on. By ordinary feelings I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the tolerance of private feelings. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “Three Obediences and Four Virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of an organization for carrying on affairs.
