① Taking the “self” as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like the members of a group all standing on the same plane. Rather, they are like ripples on water, spreading outward circle by circle: the farther they spread, the more distant and the thinner they become. Here we encounter a basic characteristic of Chinese social structure. What Confucianism studies most carefully is *lun*—human relationships. What is *lun*? My explanation is that it is the differentiated order of the successive ripples that arise among those people who, extending outward from oneself, enter into social relations with oneself. The *Shiming* also says under the character *lun* 沦: “It is *lun* 伦; the patterns on water follow one another in ordered sequence.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once said that all meanings having *lun* 仑 as a common denominator are connected; “what they jointly express is a sense of pattern, category, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The ten *lun* discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—spirits and gods, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, close and distant, ranks and rewards, husband and wife, affairs of government, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to gradations. “Not losing its *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order with graded differences. To us today, how can concrete social relations such as spirits and gods, ruler and minister, father and son, and husband and wife be placed in the same category as abstract relative positions such as noble and base, intimate and distant, near and far, superior and inferior? In fact, within our traditional social structure, the most basic concept—the guiding framework in the network formed by human intercourse—is precisely a differential order, that is, *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: “Affection for one’s kin, respect for the honorable, deference to elders, and distinction between men and women—these cannot be changed along with the people.” The meaning is that the framework of this social structure cannot be changed; what changes are only the matters carried out by making use of this framework.

③ What Confucius emphasized most was the word “extend” in the outward expansion of ripples on water. He first acknowledged a self, the self from which one extends to others. This self must be restrained by ritual propriety; to overcome the self is to cultivate the self. Following the concentric circles of ethical relations, one can then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “A man who is filial to his parents and respectful to his elder brothers will seldom be inclined to offend his superiors; one who is not inclined to offend his superiors yet is fond of making trouble has never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the five relationships as “the universal Way under Heaven.” Because, in this kind of social structure, one moves from the self to all under Heaven by extending outward circle by circle, Mencius said that he was merely “good at extending.”

④ In this highly elastic network, there is always, at every time and place, a “self” at the center. This is not individualism, but egoism. The individual is spoken of in relation to the group: the member in relation to the group. Under individualism, on the one hand there is the idea of equality, meaning that the positions of all members within the same group are equal and that the individual cannot infringe upon the rights of everyone else; on the other hand there is the idea of constitutionalism, meaning that the group cannot obliterate the individual, but can control the individual only over that portion of rights which the individual is willing to surrender. These ideas must first presuppose the existence of the group. In traditional Chinese thought we did not have this whole set of ideas, because what we had was egoism: a doctrine in which all values take the “self” as their center.

⑤ Egoism is not limited to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair even if it benefited all under Heaven; even Confucianism should be included within it. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu neglected the relativity and elasticity of egoism. He was too rigid-minded, clinging obstinately to the self and refusing to let go. Confucius knew how to extend from the self to others, but even when this extension reached to the four seas, the center still remained the self. Confucius said: “To govern by virtue is like the Pole Star: it stays in its place, and the multitude of stars pay court to it.” This is a very good metaphor for the differential mode of association. The self is always the center, like the Pole Star that does not shift with the seasons, and all other people revolve around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus had a group transcending the individual—he had his Kingdom of Heaven—so he could sacrifice himself in order to fulfill that Kingdom. Confucius was not so.

⑥ Zigong said: “If there were one who broadly bestowed benefits upon the people and was able to help the multitude, what would you say of him? Could he be called humane?” Confucius said: “Why stop at humaneness? He would surely be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would have found this difficult. As for the humane man, wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to attain success himself, he helps others to attain success. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy may be called the method of humaneness.”

⑦ In Confucius’ moral system, he would never depart from the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks it in himself; the petty man seeks it in others.” For this reason, he could not, like Jesus, love all under Heaven universally, even loving his enemies and asking God to forgive those who killed him—for these things do not start from the self as center. What about Confucius? Someone asked: “What do you think of repaying resentment with virtue?” Confucius said: “Then with what would one repay virtue? Repay resentment with uprightness, and repay virtue with virtue.” This is the hierarchy of the differential order, and Confucius would never let it go. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, use the small self to deal with every situation. Rather, he expanded or contracted this moral sphere according to need. He was not like Jesus, or like China’s Mo Di, who, once they extended outward, could not draw back.
