1. Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle straw in the fields: several stalks of rice straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a load.

2. In China, this expression is very ambiguous.

3. This network is like a spider’s web: it has a center, namely oneself.

4. The concept of the “citizen” cannot exist without first having the “state.”

5. A group of people mutually dependent in life cannot seek survival singly and scattered in the hills and forests.

6. The idea of a constitution corresponds to the Western idea of public office.

7. This social group is united for the birth and upbringing of children.

8. On the father’s side, however, it can extend very far. A Chia of five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the cohesive force.

10. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. “Those at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “members of the house” may refer to a large number of uncles, nephews, and cousins; “our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle and toward whom one wants to show intimacy. The range of “our own people” can expand or contract according to time and place; it can become so large as to be beyond counting—truly, all under Heaven can become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I call a “differential mode of association,” a “network composed of one private tie after another.” This pattern is different from the “organizational mode of association” of the modern West.

13. But now it seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

14. In structure, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equal to the family. For the basis of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, a clan is an organization for the undertaking of enterprises; when further expanded, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to conduct various enterprises, thus making the basic Chia clan-like in character.

17. The Chinese Chia is an organization for the undertaking of enterprises, and the size of the Chia is determined by the size of the enterprise.

18. Since our Chia is a continuous enterprise group, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. It is vertical, not horizontal.

19. In using this analogy, I am trying to make more concrete a pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system belongs to the organizational mode of association, but it is incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward without limit to include an infinite number of people—past, present, and future. Our common saying, “one cousinship extends three thousand li,” expresses just this idea; in fact, “three thousand li” merely indicates its vast extent.

22. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people under Heaven who can recognize exactly the same set of relatives. Two brothers, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is generally true of our kinship circle. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, cousins such as Lin Daiyu on the father’s sister’s side and Xue Baochai on the mother’s sister’s side could live there; later there were still more—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. Whoever could be connected as kin could be included. But once the balance of power changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrank into a small cluster.

24. Taking the self as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like the members of an organization all standing on the same plane. Rather, they are like ripples on the water, pushed outward circle by circle; the farther they extend, the more attenuated they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. In the “Ji Tong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*, the ten *lun*—between spirits and men, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, close and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, political affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, close and distant. *Lun* is an order of gradations.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “Those who are filial and fraternal yet fond of offending their superiors are few indeed; those who are not fond of offending their superiors yet fond of making disorder have never existed.” From the self to the Chia, from the Chia to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a single path. In the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the Five Relations are treated as “the universal Way under Heaven.”

27. An organization is a “reality” transcending the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot concretely point to some physical object and say: this is the organization. It is a bundle of relations among persons, a force controlling the behavior of each individual, an object upon which the lives of its members depend, a common will prior to any individual and yet inseparable from individuals.

28. “Minister,” or “agent,” is a basic concept in a society of the organizational mode of association. The pastor who carries out the will of God is a Minister; the official who carries out the authority of the organization is also a Minister. Both are “agents,” not God or the organization itself. The distinction between God and pastor, between state and government, must not be confused.

29. The social range constituted by extension outward from the self consists of one private tie after another, each cord being maintained by a moral element. The social range is pushed outward from the self, and in this process of extension there are various routes. The most basic is kinship: parent-child and sibling relations. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect.

30. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the married pair. This social group is united for the birth and upbringing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a social group cannot be absent.
