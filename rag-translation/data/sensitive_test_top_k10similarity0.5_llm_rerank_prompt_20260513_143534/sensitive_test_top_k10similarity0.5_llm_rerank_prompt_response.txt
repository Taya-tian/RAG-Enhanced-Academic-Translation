1. Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a carrying load.

2. In China, this expression is extremely ambiguous.

3. This network is like a spider’s web: it has a center, and that center is oneself.

4. The concept of the “citizen” presupposes the concept of the “state.”

5. A group of people mutually dependent in their livelihood cannot survive alone and scattered in the mountains and forests.

6. The concept of a constitution corresponds to the Western concept of public office.

7. The union of this social group is for the birth and rearing of children.

8. On the father’s side, however, it may extend very far. A family with five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the two sexes is the force of cohesion.

10. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. “The one at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “members of the family house” may refer to a large number of uncles and nephews; “one of our own” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle as a gesture of intimacy. The range of “one of our own” can expand or contract according to time and place; it may become so large as to be beyond counting—indeed, all under Heaven may become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I have called a “differential mode of association”: it is a “network formed by personal ties, one strand after another.” This pattern differs from the modern Western “organizational mode of association.”

13. But as I see it now, this term is not quite appropriate. A more accurate name for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage-family.”

14. In its structure, the lineage-family includes the family; the smallest lineage-family may even be identical with the family. For the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation—the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, a clan is an organization for carrying out undertakings; if further enlarged, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical norms of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thus making the basic Chia clan-like in character.

17. The Chinese Chia is an organization for undertaking affairs, and its size is determined by the size of the undertakings it has to carry out.

18. Since our Chia is a continuing group organized around common undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal.

19. In using this metaphor, I am trying to make more concrete a certain pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system belongs to the organizational mode of association, but it is entirely incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward without limit, including persons of the past, the present, and the future. The common saying “one cousinship reaches three thousand li” expresses just this idea; in fact, “three thousand li” merely indicates the breadth of its extension.

22. In the kinship system, we all have parents; but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two persons under Heaven whose recognized kin can be exactly the same. Two brothers, to be sure, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is much the same as our kinship circles. In the Grand View Garden of the Jia family, for example, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin through a paternal aunt, and Xue Baochai, a cousin through a maternal aunt; later there were still more—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. Anyone who could be connected by kinship could be included. But once the family’s power changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle contracted into a small group.

24. Taking the self as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like the members of an organization standing all on the same plane. Rather, they are like ripples on water, spreading outward circle by circle; the farther they extend, the more distant and the thinner they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The ten *lun* discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, close and distant kin, rank and reward, husband and wife, political affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, close and distant kin. *Lun* is an order of differentiated ranks.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “Those who are filial and fraternal, yet fond of offending their superiors, are few indeed; those who are not fond of offending their superiors, yet fond of making disorder, have never existed.” From the self to the Chia, from the Chia to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. In the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the five *lun* are treated as “the universal Way under Heaven.”

27. An organization is a “reality” transcending the individual; it is not a tangible thing. We cannot point to some concrete object with physical form and say, “This is the organization.” It is a bundle of interpersonal relations, a force controlling the behavior of each individual, an object upon which the lives of its constituent members depend, a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot exist apart from individuals.

28. The “agent,” or *Minister*, is a basic concept in a society of the organizational mode of association. The pastor who carries out the will of God is a Minister; the official who carries out the authority of the organization is also a Minister. Both are “agents,” not God or the organization itself. The distinction between God and pastor, between state and government, must not be confused.

29. The social sphere extended outward from the self is made up of personal ties, one strand after another, each maintained by a moral element. The social sphere is pushed outward from the “self,” and in this process there are various routes. The most basic is kinship: parent-child and sibling relations. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect.

30. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. The union of this social group is for the birth and rearing of children. In a society where individuals bear the task of producing and raising children, such a social group will not be lacking.
