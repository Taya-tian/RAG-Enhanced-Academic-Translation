**Group Two: Chinese and Western Egoism and Individualism  
(Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”)**

① Taking the “self” as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like members of a group all standing on the same plane. Rather, they are like ripples on the surface of water, spreading outward circle by circle: the farther they extend, the more distant and the thinner they become. Here we encounter the basic characteristic of Chinese social structure. What Confucianism has studied most carefully is *renlun*—human relations. What is *lun*? My explanation is that it is the differential ordering of the successive circles of ripples formed among those people who enter into social relations with oneself as they extend outward from oneself. The *Shiming*, under the character *lun* 沦, also says: “*Lun* means order; the patterns of water follow one another and have order.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once said that all meanings containing *lun* 仑 as a common element are connected, “jointly expressing the ideas of pattern, category, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The “ten *lun*” discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—spirits and men, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, close and distant kin, rank and reward, husband and wife, political affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to gradations. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, close and distant. *Lun* is an order of differences in degree. To us today, how can such concrete social relations as spirits and men, ruler and minister, father and son, and husband and wife be placed on the same level as such abstract relative positions as noble and base, close and distant, near and far, and superior and inferior? In fact, in our traditional social structure, the most basic concept—the guiding order in the network constituted by human interaction—is precisely a differential order, that is, *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: “Loving one’s kin as kin, honoring the honorable, respecting elders as elders, and distinguishing between men and women—these are things that cannot be changed together with the people.” The meaning is that the framework of this social structure cannot be changed; what changes are only the things done by making use of this framework.

③ What Confucius paid the greatest attention to was the word “extend”—the outward expansion of ripples on water. He first acknowledged a self, the self from which one extends consideration to others. This self must be subdued by ritual propriety; subduing the self is self-cultivation. Following the concentric circles of human relations and norms, one can then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “Those who are filial and fraternal, yet like to offend their superiors, are few indeed; those who do not like to offend their superiors, yet like to create disorder, have never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is one continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the five relations as “the universal Way under Heaven.” Because in this kind of social structure one moves from the self to all under Heaven circle by circle, Mencius said that he was simply “good at extending” his feelings to others.

④ In this highly elastic network, there is always, at every time and place, a “self” serving as the center. This is not individualism, but egoism. The individual is spoken of in relation to the group: the member in relation to the whole. Under individualism, on the one hand there is the idea of equality, meaning that the positions of all members within the same group are equal and that the individual cannot infringe upon the rights of the group; on the other hand there is the idea of constitutionalism, meaning that the group cannot obliterate the individual, and can control the individual only within that portion of rights which the individual is willing to surrender. These ideas must first assume the existence of the group. In traditional Chinese thought we do not have this set of ideas, because what we have is egoism: a doctrine in which all values take the “self” as their center.

⑤ Egoism is not limited to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair from his body to benefit the world; even the Confucians should be included within it. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu ignored the relativity and elasticity of egoism. He was too rigid-minded, clinging stubbornly to the self and refusing to let go. Confucius, by contrast, knew how to extend from the self to others; yet even when this extension reached to the four seas, the center still remained the self. The Master said: “He who governs by virtue may be compared to the North Star: it remains in its place, while all the other stars revolve around it.” This is a very good metaphor for the differential mode of association: the self is always the center, like the unmoving North Star, while all other people revolve around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus had a group transcending the individual; he had his Kingdom of Heaven, and so he could sacrifice himself to fulfill the Kingdom of Heaven. But Confucius was not like this.

⑥ Zigong said: “If there were one who could broadly confer benefits on the people and bring aid to the multitude, what would you say of him? Could he be called humane?” The Master said: “Why stop at humanity? He would surely be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would have found this difficult. As for the humane man: wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to achieve success himself, he helps others achieve success. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy—this may be called the method of humanity.”

⑦ In Confucius’s moral system, he never permitted one to leave the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks it in himself; the petty man seeks it in others.” For this reason, he could not, like Jesus, love all under Heaven universally, even loving his enemies and praying to God to forgive those who killed him—these things do not begin from the self as center. What about Confucius? Someone asked: “To repay hatred with virtue—how would that be?” The Master said: “Then with what would one repay virtue? Repay hatred with uprightness, and repay virtue with virtue.” This is the hierarchy of the differential order, and Confucius never relaxed it. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, respond to all situations with the small self. He expanded or contracted the scope of morality according to need. He was not like Jesus, or like Mo Di in China, who, once they extended love outward, could not draw it back.
