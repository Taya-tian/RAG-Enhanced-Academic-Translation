**Group Four: The Chinese and Western Family (Chapter Six, “The Family-Clan”)**

① What I wish to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “expanded family.” In both expressions the main term is “family,” with an adjective such as “large” or “expanded” added to indicate a formal difference between Chinese and Western families, which are thus assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small family-clan.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the difference between the Chinese and Western “family” in terms of structural principle. What we ordinarily call the difference between the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large family,” while a family of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot therefore be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two are different.

③ In anthropology, the concept of the family has a clear definition: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. This group is formed for the begetting and rearing of children. In any society where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, in the case of each individual family, is short-term. When the children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage the affairs of begetting and rearing children of their own, generation after generation. The family as a group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools, states, and such groups are not temporary; though in fact they are not permanent, they are not provisional, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the beginning prepare for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring child-rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The Institution of Reproduction*.

④ But in any culture, the family group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature and remains a group composed of parents and children, then at the time of its formation and again after the children have grown, there is a period when it is simply a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife may engage in economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can undertake are greatly limited. Any matter requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be carried on by other groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate group; as I have explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can carry on are few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, extend outward along the differential sequence of kinship relations. Those who make up what I have called this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line of expansion is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line; that is, it includes only the patrilineal side. With few exceptions, the Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, expansion may go very far. A Chia of five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A group organized according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a special name in anthropology: the clan. In structure, our Chia is a clan. But it is not quite the same as what we ordinarily call the Tsu, for what we call the Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a group of groups. It is for this reason that I here propose the term “small family-clan.” The small family-clan and the large family-clan are the same in structural principle; their difference lies in quantity, in size. This is why I do not wish to use “large family,” but instead use “small family-clan.” The difference of a single word reveals the structural nature of this group.

⑦ Structurally, the family-clan includes the family; the smallest family-clan may even be identical with the family. For the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The family-clan is an extension from the basis of the family. But the family included within the family-clan is only one ring in the social circle. We cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can we say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the family-clan includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on enterprises; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various enterprises, thus making the basic Chia clan-like in character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the family-clan; on the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on so many enterprises, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small parent-child combination, but must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group therefore cannot be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous; it must not split apart when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of the clan. The clan is by nature long-term, just as our Chia is. To call this group of ours a small family-clan also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary nature of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on enterprises, and the size of the Chia is determined by the size of the enterprise. If the enterprise is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the enterprise is large and exceeds what husband and wife can undertake, then brothers, uncles, and other patrilineal kin may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary so greatly. But whatever the differences in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ To use a procreative group to bear many other functions changes the content of the relations among the members of the group. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly carry on the work of procreation, while children are supporting figures in the group; when they grow up, they leave it. Political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other corporate groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Since husband and wife become the main axis, the feelings between the sexes become the cohesive force. The development of sexual affection makes the family the center from which comfort in life is obtained. In my book *American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous enterprise group, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are compelled by the needs of the enterprise to exclude ordinary feelings. By “ordinary feelings” I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No enterprise can be separated from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private sentiment. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; for women there are the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children there is an emphasis on responsibility and obedience. All these are characteristics of an enterprise group.
