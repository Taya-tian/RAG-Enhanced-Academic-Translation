4. The idea of the “citizen” must first presuppose the existence of a “state.”

5. A group of people whose lives depend upon one another cannot survive alone and scattered in the mountains and forests.

8. On the father’s side, however, it can extend very far. A household of five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

9. Husband and wife become the principal axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force of cohesion.

10. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. “Those at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “the family gate” may refer to a large group of uncles and nephews; “our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle and toward whom one wishes to show intimacy. The range of “our own people” can expand or contract according to time and place; it may become so large as to be beyond counting—indeed, all under heaven may become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I have called the “differential mode of association,” a “network formed by one strand after another of personal relationships.” This pattern is different from the “organizational mode of association” found in the modern West.

13. But as I now see it, this term is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage-family.”

14. Structurally, the lineage-family includes the family; the smallest lineage-family may even be identical with the family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relationship—the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, a clan is a corporate organization for common undertakings; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the moral order of kinship to organize social groups and to conduct various common undertakings, thereby making the basic family clan-like in character.

17. The Chinese family is a corporate organization, and its size is determined by the size of the undertaking it has to manage.

18. Since our family is a continuing corporate community, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. It is vertical, not horizontal.

19. I use this metaphor in order to make more concrete a certain pattern of human relations in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system belongs to the organizational mode of association, but it is wholly incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward without limit, including people of the past, the present, and the future. The saying “one cousinship reaches three thousand li” expresses just this idea; in fact, “three thousand li” merely indicates the breadth of its possible extension.

22. In the kinship system, we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people under heaven whose recognized kin are exactly the same. Two brothers, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is like our kinship circle. In the Grand View Garden of the Jia family, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin through a paternal aunt, and Xue Baochai, a cousin through a maternal aunt; later there were still more—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. Anyone who could be connected as kin could be included. But once power and influence changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrank into a small cluster.

24. Taking the self as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations one forms with others are not like members of an organization all standing on the same plane. Rather, they are like ripples on the surface of water, spreading outward circle by circle; the farther they spread, the more distant and the thinner they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. In the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*, the ten *lun*—between ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and humble, close and distant kin, ranks and rewards, husband and wife, affairs of government, elder and younger, and superior and inferior—all refer to gradations. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father from son, near from far, close from distant. *Lun* is an order of graded differences.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “Few are those who are filial and fraternal yet fond of offending their superiors; none are those who are not fond of offending their superiors yet fond of making disorder.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under heaven—this is a single continuous path. In the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the five relationships are taken as “the universal Way under heaven.”

27. A group is a “reality” that stands above the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot take out some concrete object and say, “This is the group.” It is a bundle of relationships among persons, a force controlling the behavior of individuals, an object upon which the lives of its members depend, a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot exist apart from individuals...

28. “Minister,” or “agent,” is a basic concept in a society organized according to the organizational mode of association. The pastor who carries out the will of God is a minister; the official who carries out the authority of the group is also a minister. Both are agents, not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and the pastor, and between the state and the government, must not be confused.

29. The social sphere pushed outward from the self is made up of strand after strand of personal connections, each strand being maintained by a particular moral element. The social sphere extends outward from the self, and in the process of extension there are various routes. The most basic is kinship: the parent-child relation and the relation among siblings. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal deference.

30. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. This social group is joined together for the birth and rearing of children. In a society where individuals assume the task of bearing and raising children, such a group will not be lacking.
