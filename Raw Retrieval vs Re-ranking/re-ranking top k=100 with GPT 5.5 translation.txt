① What I should like to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” In these terms the substantive word is “family”; adjectives such as “small” or “large” are added to indicate formal differences between Chinese and Western “families,” assumed to be the same in nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group in Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the principle of structure, the difference between the Chia in Chinese and Western societies. The difference between what we ordinarily call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large family,” while a household of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” In number the former is larger than the latter, but in structure the latter is more complex than the former. The principles used in the two are different.

③ In anthropology the concept of the family has a definite definition: it is a procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” indicates its structure; “procreative” indicates its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. The group is formed for the bearing and rearing of children. In any society where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a group cannot be absent. But the procreative function, for each individual family, is short-term. When the children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage their own affairs of bearing and rearing children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states are not temporary; though in fact they are not eternal, they are not provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the beginning prepare for its own ending. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in any culture, the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to the group formed by parents and children, then at the time of its formation and again after the children have grown up there is a period in which it is merely a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife certainly carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they manage are greatly limited. All affairs requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be managed by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are also few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who constitute what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. But structurally the line along which expansion takes place is limited. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilateral line; that is, it includes only the patrilineal side. With few exceptions, the Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law at the same time. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of an outside Chia. On the father’s side, however, expansion may go very far. A Chia in which five generations live under the same roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to the principle of unilateral kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not exactly the same as what we ordinarily call the Tsu, because what we call the Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group of social groups. For this reason, I have here proposed the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity, or size. This is why I do not wish to use “large family,” but instead use “small lineage.” The difference of one word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family. The smallest lineage may even be equal to a family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended out from the foundation of the family. But the family included in the lineage is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Though the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for undertakings; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is just like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical norms of kinship to organize social groups and manage various undertakings, thus making the basic Chia clan-like in character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that, in order to manage so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children, but must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous; it must not split apart when an individual grows up, nor end when an individual dies. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of the clan. The clan is by nature long-term, just like our Chia. To call this kind of social group of ours a “small lineage” also indicates this long-term quality, in contrast to the temporary nature of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for undertakings, and the size of the Chia is determined by the size of the undertaking. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife alone can bear, brothers, paternal uncles, and other relatives may all gather into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the differences in size may be, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilateral, differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly manage the affairs of procreation; children play secondary roles in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. For them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the family’s sphere. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force that binds the group together. The development of sexual affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous group for undertakings, its main axes lie between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; they are vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are compelled by the needs of the undertaking to exclude ordinary affection. By “ordinary affection” I mean something contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can escape considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must speak of discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there is domestic law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of a group organized for undertakings.
