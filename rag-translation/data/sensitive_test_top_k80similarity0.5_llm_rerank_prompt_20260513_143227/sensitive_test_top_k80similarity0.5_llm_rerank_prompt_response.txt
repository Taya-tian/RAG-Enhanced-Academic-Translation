9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes is the cohesive force.

10. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. “Those at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “the family gate” may refer to a large group of uncles and nephews; “one of our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle and toward whom one wishes to show intimacy. The range of “our own people” is elastic according to time and place; it may be enlarged beyond counting, so that truly all under Heaven may become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I have called a “differential mode of association,” a “network formed by strand after strand of personal relationships.” This pattern is different from the “group mode of association” of the modern West.

13. But now it seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more exact term for the basic social group of Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage.”

14. Structurally, the lineage includes the family, and the smallest lineage may be identical with the family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, the clan is a corporate organization; when further expanded, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to combine social groups and manage various undertakings, thus giving the basic Chia a clan-like character.

17. The Chinese Chia is a corporate organization, and its size is determined by the size of the undertakings it carries on.

18. Since our Chia is a continuing corporate community, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal.

19. In using this metaphor, I wish to make more concrete a certain pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call it the group mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system is of the group-mode type, but it is entirely incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward without limit, including persons past, present, and future. Our common saying, “a cousinship reaches three thousand li,” expresses precisely this meaning; in fact, “three thousand li” merely indicates the breadth of its extension.

22. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two persons under Heaven whose recognized kin are completely identical. Two brothers do, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is generally true of our kinship circle. In the Grand View Garden of the Jia family, for example, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin through a paternal aunt, and Xue Baochai, a cousin through a maternal aunt; later there were even more, such as Baoqin and Xiuyan. Anyone who could be linked as a relative could be accommodated. But once power and influence changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle contracted into a small cluster.

24. Taking the self as the centre, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like members of a group all standing on the same plane; rather, they are like ripples on the water, pushing outward circle by circle—the farther they extend, the more distant and thinner they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinction. The ten *lun* discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, close and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, political affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order of graded distinctions.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “Those who are filial and fraternal yet fond of offending their superiors are rare; those who are not fond of offending their superiors yet fond of making disorder have never existed.” From the self to the Chia, from the Chia to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the five relationships as “the universal Way under Heaven.”

27. A group is a “reality” above the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot point to some concrete object with form and say, “This is the group.” It is a bundle of human relations, a force controlling the behavior of each individual, an entity on which the lives of its constituent members depend, a common will that precedes any individual yet cannot be separated from individuals.

28. The “agent,” or Minister, is a fundamental concept in a society of the group mode of association. The pastor who carries out the will of God is a Minister; the official who carries out the authority of the group is also a Minister. Both are “agents,” not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and pastor, state and government, cannot be confused.

29. The social sphere extended outward from the self is made up of strand after strand of personal connection, each strand maintained by a moral element. The social sphere is pushed outward from the self, and in this process there are various routes. The most fundamental is kinship: parent-child and sibling relations. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect.

30. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. The union of this social group is for the birth and upbringing of children. In a society where the tasks of bearing and rearing children are borne by individuals, such a social group cannot be lacking.
