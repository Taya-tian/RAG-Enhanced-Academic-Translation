**Group Four: The Chinese and Western Chia**  
**Chapter Six, “The Lineage”**

① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group ordinarily called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” The core of these terms is “family,” with an adjective such as “small” or “large” added to indicate the formal difference between the Chinese and Western “family,” assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite proper. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, in terms of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western chia. The distinction we commonly make between the large-family and the small-family is certainly not one of size, not a matter of the number of persons included in the group, but one of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions for being a “large-family,” while a household of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small-family.” In numbers, the former is larger than the latter; but structurally, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two cases are different.

③ In anthropology the concept of the family has a definite definition: it is a procreative group consisting of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. This group is united for the birth and nurture of children. In any society in which individuals undertake the task of bearing and rearing children, such groups will not be lacking. But the procreative function, considered in each individual family, is short-term. When the children grow up, they leave the nurture of their parents and go on to conduct their own affairs of begetting and rearing children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not entirely like ordinary social groups. Schools and states are not temporary groups; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, for their functions are long-term. Since the function of the family is procreation and nurture, it must be prepared, from the beginning, for its own termination. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Procreation*.

④ Yet in every culture the family group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change in nature, and remains limited to the group formed by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is only a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation; but the affairs they can conduct are greatly limited. Matters requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs that this group can conduct are few, chiefly the begetting and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the chia has no strict corporate boundary. The members of this group may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members forming what I have called this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line of expansion is restricted. The Chinese chia expands along a unilineal line: it includes only the paternal side. With few exceptions, the chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are both people of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, expansion can go very far. A chia of “five generations under one roof” may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to this principle of unilineal kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our chia is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a lineage or clan, because what we call a lineage is composed of many chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity and size. This is why I do not wish to use “large-family,” but instead use “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family. The smallest lineage may be equal to a family, because the foundation of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended from the basis of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit by itself. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our chia is precisely like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical norms of kinship to organize social groups and to conduct various enterprises, thus making the basic chia clan-like in nature. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on these many undertakings, the structure of the chia cannot be limited to the small grouping of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, all political, economic, and religious affairs require long-term continuity. This basic social group absolutely cannot be temporary in the way the Western family is. The chia must be continuous: it must not split apart when an individual grows up, nor come to an end when an individual dies. Thus the nature of the chia becomes that of the lineage. The clan is by nature long-term, just like our chia. To call this kind of social group a “small lineage” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese chia is an organization for carrying on undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be enough to manage it, and the chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, then brothers and paternal uncles may all be gathered into one large chia. This explains why the size of the chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is the unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family group, husband and wife form the principal axis. Husband and wife jointly conduct the affairs of procreation and nurture; children play secondary roles in the group, and when they grow up they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups; they do not fall within the province of the family. Husband and wife become the principal axis, and affection between the sexes is the force of cohesion. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes their family the center for obtaining comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our chia is a continuous social group for carrying on undertakings, its principal axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the principal axis, is not temporary, both axes are made, by the needs of the undertaking, to exclude ordinary affection. By “ordinary affection” I mean something contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can escape considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feelings. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of a social group organized for carrying on undertakings.
