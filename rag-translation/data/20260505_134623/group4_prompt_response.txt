**Group Four: Chinese and Western Forms of the “Family”  
Chapter Six, “The Family-Clan”**

① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I referred to it as the “expanded family.” In these terms the central concept is “family,” to which an adjective such as “small” or “large” is added in order to indicate a formal distinction between Chinese and Western families, assumed to be identical in nature. Looking at the matter now, however, I find this inappropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group of Chinese rural society would be the “small family-clan.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the difference between Chinese and Western forms of the “family” in terms of structural principles. The distinction between what we ordinarily call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but rather a matter of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions for being a “large family,” while a household consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles by which the two are organized are different.

③ In anthropology, the concept of the family has a precise definition: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation and rearing” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married pair. The group is formed for the birth and upbringing of children. In any society where individuals bear the responsibility for producing and rearing children, such a group is indispensable. Yet the procreative function of each individual family is short-term. Once children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to undertake the task of producing and rearing children of their own, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite like ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not eternal in fact, but neither are they temporary in character, because the functions they perform are long-term. Since the family takes procreation and child-rearing as its function, it must be prepared for its own termination from the moment it begins. The aim of raising children is precisely to bring child-rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in *The System of Reproduction*.

④ In any culture, however, the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation and child-rearing. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end simply because the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains a group limited to parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and again after the children have grown up, there is a period during which it consists solely of the conjugal pair. Husband and wife may indeed engage in economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Any undertaking that requires the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by some other social group.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the range of affairs this group can manage is quite limited, consisting chiefly of the procreation and upbringing of children. In Chinese rural society, however, the *jia* has no strict corporate boundaries. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship relations. The members constituting what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which the structure expands is itself restricted. The Chinese *jia* expands along a unilineal path: it includes only the patrilineal side. With a few exceptions, the *jia* cannot simultaneously include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are both persons of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, expansion may extend very far. A household of five generations living under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our *jia* is a clan. Yet it is not exactly the same as what we ordinarily call a lineage, because what we call a lineage is composed of many *jia*; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small family-clan.” The small family-clan and the large family-clan are identical in structural principle; they differ only in quantity, in size. This is why I am unwilling to use the term “large family” and instead use “small family-clan.” A difference of a single word reveals the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the family-clan includes the family; the smallest family-clan may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relationship, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The family-clan is extended outward from the foundation of the family. But the family contained within the family-clan is only one ring within the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit in itself; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Although the family-clan includes the function of procreation and child-rearing, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, a clan is an organization for collective undertakings; if further enlarged, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex functions—political, economic, religious, and so forth. Our *jia* is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted a differential mode of association and used the ethical order of kinship to organize social groups and conduct various undertakings, thereby transforming the basic *jia* into something clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be entrusted to the family-clan. On the other hand, we may also say that because so many undertakings must be carried out, the structure of the *jia* cannot remain limited to the small grouping of parents and children, but must be expanded. Moreover, all political, economic, and religious affairs require long-term continuity. This basic social group therefore cannot be temporary in the manner of the Western family. The *jia* must be continuous: it must not divide when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the *jia* becomes that of the clan. The clan is by nature long-lasting, just like our *jia*. To call this social group of ours a “small family-clan” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary nature of the family.

⑨ The Chinese *jia* is an organization for collective undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient to handle it, and the *jia* may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, then brothers, uncles, and other patrilineal kin may all gather within one large *jia*. This explains why the size of the *jia* in our rural society can vary so greatly. But however great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, patrilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to assume many other functions, the content of the relationships among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Together they carry out the work of procreation and child-rearing. Children play supporting roles within this group, and once they have grown up they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are assumed by other groups and do not fall within the responsibilities of the family. Since husband and wife constitute the main axis, affection between the sexes becomes the force of cohesion. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes the family the center from which comfort in life is obtained. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the *jia* differs markedly in this respect. Since our *jia* is a continuous social group organized for collective undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes exclude ordinary sentiment because of the demands of collective undertakings. By “ordinary sentiment” I mean sentiment as contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To pursue efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “Three Obediences and Four Virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. All of these are characteristics of a social group organized for collective undertakings.
