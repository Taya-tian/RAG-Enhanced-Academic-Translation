**Group One: Chinese and Western Social Relations (Chapter 4, “The Differential Mode of Association”)**

① Western society is in some respects like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a shoulder-load. Every stalk of firewood within the whole load belongs to a definite bundle, sheaf, and handful. Each stalk can also be identified with other stalks in the same handful, the same sheaf, or the same bundle; the divisions are clear and not confused. In society, these units are groups. When I say that Western social organization resembles bundled firewood, I mean to indicate that it is often composed of one group after another, each made up of a number of people. A group has definite boundaries: who belongs to the group and who stands outside it cannot be ambiguous, but must be clearly distinguished. Those within the group form a body, and their relation to the group is the same. If there are subdivisions or ranks within the same group, these too are stipulated in advance. My analogy of bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may participate in several groups, whereas it is of course impossible for the same stalk of firewood to appear in several different sheaves. This is where people differ from firewood. I use this metaphor simply to make more concrete a certain pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call this the **group mode of association**.

② In the West, the family is a clearly bounded group. If a friend writes to you saying that he will “bring his family” to visit, he knows quite well which persons will be coming with him. In China, however, such a statement is extremely vague. In Britain and America, the family includes the man himself, his wife, and their minor children. If he were coming only with his wife, he would not say that he was bringing his “family.” In China, although expressions such as “may your entire household honor us with your presence” are common, very few people can say precisely whom this “household” is supposed to include.

③ Since we have mentioned terminology, the Chinese word **jia**—“family” or “home”—may be said to be the most elastic of all. “The one at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “members of the family clan” may refer to a large group including uncles and nephews; and “one of our own” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s circle in order to express intimacy. The scope of “one of our own” expands and contracts according to time and place. At its largest it may be so broad as to be beyond counting—indeed, “all under Heaven may become one family.”

④ Why should the term for our most basic social unit be so vague and indeterminate? In my view, this reflects the fact that our social structure itself differs from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood tied up neatly one by one; rather, it is like the ripples produced when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water, spreading outward in circle after circle. Each person is the center of the circles generated by his or her social influence. Those reached by the ripples of these circles enter into relations with that person. The circles that each individual mobilizes at a given time and place are not necessarily the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society have precisely this character of concentric ripples formed by a thrown stone. Kinship relations are social relations generated on the basis of the facts of birth and marriage. The network formed through birth and marriage can extend outward without limit, encompassing an infinite number of people—past, present, and future. The common saying “one cousinship extends for three thousand li” expresses this idea; in fact, “three thousand li” merely signifies vast breadth. This network is like a spider’s web, with a center, namely oneself. Each of us has such a web extending outward through kinship relations, but no two webs cover exactly the same people. People in a society may use the same system to identify their kin; what is shared is only the system itself. The system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts of relationship. When we use this system to identify concrete relatives, however, the persons identified differ from individual to individual. Within the kinship system, we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go one step further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized kin can be completely identical. Two brothers, to be sure, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Thus, as far as the network of social relations connected through kinship is concerned, it is individualized. Each network has an “I” at its center, and the center of each network is different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of territorial relations. The modern **baojia** system is characterized by the group mode of association, but this is utterly incompatible with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each household takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around it; this circle is the “neighborhood.” When there is a wedding or other happy occasion, neighbors are invited to drink; when a child is born, red eggs are distributed; when there is a funeral, they come to assist with encoffining and carrying the coffin. It is an institution of mutual aid in everyday life. Yet it is not a fixed group, but a range or sphere. The size of this sphere depends on the strength and influence of the center. The neighborhood of a powerful household may extend throughout the entire village, whereas that of a poor household may include only two or three adjacent families. This is much like our kinship circles. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, for example, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin through the paternal aunt’s line, and Xue Baochai, a cousin through the maternal aunt’s line; later still came others such as Baoqin and Xiuyan. Anyone for whom some kinship tie could be claimed could be accommodated. But when power changes, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle contracts into a small cluster. In the extreme case, it may be like Su Qin returning home in destitution: “his wife did not treat him as a husband, nor did his sister-in-law treat him as an uncle.” The differential mode of association in China’s traditional structure possesses precisely this capacity for expansion and contraction. In the countryside, a family may be very small; but among wealthy landlords and bureaucratic strata, it may become as large as a small state. Chinese people are especially sensitive to the warmth and coldness of worldly relations precisely because these highly elastic social circles expand or shrink according to changes in the power of their center.

⑦ In Western societies, where even grown children living at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, people acknowledge the boundaries of the group. Those within the group possess definite qualifications for membership. Once those qualifications are lost, one must leave the group. For them, this is not a matter of warmth or coldness in human feeling, but a matter of rights. In Western society, what people contend for is rights; among us, by contrast, people seek to climb through relationships and to speak in terms of personal sentiment and favors.
