① What I wish to bring forward for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “Expanded Family.” The central term in these expressions is “family,” with an adjective such as “small” or “large” added to indicate a formal difference between Chinese and Western families, which are assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society should be “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, in terms of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western “family” or *chia*. The distinction between what we commonly call the large-family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions for being a “large-family”; a household with only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” In numerical terms, the former is larger than the latter, but structurally the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two cases are different.

③ The concept of family has a clear definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relationship is bilateral, including both father and mother; the children are limited to those born to the spouses. This group is formed for the birth and rearing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is undertaken by individuals, this kind of group will not be absent. But the procreative function, with respect to each individual family, is short-term. When the children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to conduct their own affairs of procreation and child-rearing, generation after generation. The family group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family group is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because the functions they perform are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must prepare for its own termination from the very beginning. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring that rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains a group limited to parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is only a union of husband and wife. To be sure, husband and wife carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation; but the affairs they conduct are greatly limited. Matters requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can conduct are few; its principal function is the begetting and rearing of children. In Chinese rural society, however, the *chia* has no strict corporate boundaries. The members of this group may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who constitute what I have called this social circle are not limited to parents and children. But the line along which it expands structurally is restricted. The Chinese *chia* expands along a unilineal line: it includes only the paternal side. With few exceptions, the *chia* cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, the son-in-law and the married daughter are both outsiders to the *chia*. On the father’s side, however, expansion can go very far. A household of five generations under one roof may include all paternal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed according to this unilineal principle of kinship has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our *chia* is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a *tsu*, or lineage, because what we call a *tsu* is composed of many *chia*; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I here propose the term “small lineage.” A small lineage and a large lineage are the same in structural principle; the difference lies in number and size. This is why I am unwilling to use the term “large-family” and instead use “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family. The smallest lineage may even be equal to a family. This is because the basis of the kinship structure is the parent-child relationship, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended outward from the basis of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring within the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the procreative function, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on affairs; when further enlarged, it can become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our *chia* is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using kinship ethics and obligations to organize social groups and to conduct various affairs, thereby transforming the basic *chia* into something clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to conduct these many affairs, the structure of the *chia* cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, all political, economic, and religious affairs require long-term continuity. This basic social group cannot be temporary, as the Western family is. The *chia* must be continuous; it must not split apart when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the *chia* becomes that of the lineage. The clan is by nature long-term, just like our *chia*. To call this kind of social group a small lineage is also to indicate this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese *chia* is an organization for carrying on affairs. Its size is determined by the scale of the affairs it undertakes. If the affairs are small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and this *chia* may be as small as a family. If the affairs are large, exceeding what husband and wife alone can bear, then brothers, uncles, and nephews may all gather into one large *chia*. This explains why the size of the *chia* in our rural society can vary greatly. But no matter how great the differences in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relationships among its members also changes. In the Western family group, husband and wife form the principal axis. Husband and wife jointly conduct the affairs of procreation; the children are supporting figures in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Among Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes becomes the force of cohesion. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes the family the center for obtaining comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑪ In our rural society, the nature of the *chia* differs markedly in this respect. Since our *chia* is a continuous group organized for carrying on affairs, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. It is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are nevertheless made, by the demands of collective affairs, to exclude ordinary feelings. By “ordinary feelings” I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No enterprise can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must speak of discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feelings. In the Chinese family there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; for women there are the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of an enterprise-oriented social group.
