4. The concept of the “citizen” cannot exist without first having the concept of the “state.”

5. A group of people whose lives depend on one another cannot seek survival singly and scattered in the mountains and forests.

6. The idea of a constitution corresponds to the Western idea of public office.

7. This social group is united for the birth and rearing of children.

8. On the father’s side, however, it can extend very far. A family with five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the cohesive force.

10. In my book *American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. “Those at home” may refer only to one’s own wife; “members of the family gate” may refer to a large number of uncles, cousins, and nephews; “one’s own people” may include anyone one wishes to draw into one’s circle in order to show intimacy. The range of “one’s own people” can expand or contract according to time and place; it may become so large as to be beyond counting—indeed, all under Heaven may become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I call a “differential mode of association,” a “network composed of one strand after another of personal connections.” This pattern differs from the modern Western “organizational mode of association.”

13. But now it seems to me that this term is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

14. Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may be equivalent to a family. For the foundation of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, a clan is an organization for carrying on collective undertakings; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the moral order of kinship to organize social groups and conduct various undertakings, thus causing the basic family to take on a clan-like character.

17. The Chinese family is an organization for carrying on undertakings; the size of the family is determined by the scale of the undertaking.

18. Since our family is a continuous corporate group, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal.

19. In using this metaphor, I am trying to make more concrete a certain pattern of human relationships in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system belongs to the organizational mode of association, but this is utterly incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward indefinitely to include an infinite number of people—past, present, and future. Our common saying, “one collateral cousinship extends three thousand li,” expresses just this idea; in fact, “three thousand li” merely indicates its vast extent.

22. In the kinship system we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized kin can be exactly the same. Two brothers may indeed have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is like our kinship circle. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin through a paternal aunt, and Xue Baochai, a cousin through a maternal aunt; later there were even more, such as Baoqin and Xiuyan. Anyone who could be connected as a relative could be included. But once power changed hands, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrank into a small cluster.

24. Taking the self as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like members of an organization all standing on the same plane; rather, they are like ripples on water, spreading outward circle by circle—the farther they spread, the more distant and attenuated they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The “ten lun” discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—between ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, close and distant kin, rank and reward, husband and wife, government affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s lun” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, close and distant. *Lun* is an order of graded distinctions.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “Those who are filial and fraternal, yet fond of offending their superiors, are few indeed; those who are not fond of offending their superiors, yet fond of making rebellion, have never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* takes the five relationships as “the universal Way under Heaven.”

27. The group is a “reality” that transcends the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot concretely produce some physical object and say: this is the group. It is a bundle of relations between persons, a force controlling the behavior of individuals, an object upon which the lives of its members depend, a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot be separated from individuals.

28. The “agent,” or Minister, is a basic concept in a society organized according to the organizational mode of association. The priest who carries out the will of God is a Minister; the official who carries out the authority of the group is also a Minister. Both are “agents,” not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and priest, between state and government, must not be confused.

29. The social sphere pushed outward from the self is made up of one personal connection after another; each strand is maintained by a moral element. This social sphere extends outward from the self, and in the process of extension there are various routes. The most basic is kinship: parent-child and sibling relations. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect.

30. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. This social group is united for the birth and rearing of children. In societies where individuals bear the responsibility for procreation and child-rearing, such groups will not be lacking.
