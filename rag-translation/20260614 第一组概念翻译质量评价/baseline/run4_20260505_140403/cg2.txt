① Taking the “self” as the center, like a stone cast into water, the social relations formed with others are not like the members of a group all standing on the same plane. Rather, they are like ripples on the water, spreading outward circle by circle: the farther they extend, the more attenuated they become. Here we encounter the basic characteristic of Chinese social structure. What our Confucian tradition has examined most carefully is *renlun*—human relationships and their ordering. What is *lun*? My interpretation is that it refers to the differential sequence of the successive ripples generated among those people who, extending outward from oneself, enter into social relations with oneself. The *Shiming* also says under the character *lun* 沦: “*Lun* means order; the patterns of water follow one another and possess order.” Mr. Pan Guangdan once remarked that all meanings sharing *lun* 仑 as a common component are interconnected: they “jointly express the ideas of pattern, category, and order” (see Pan Guangdan, “On the Character *Lun*,” *Social Research*, no. 19).

② *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The “ten *lun*” discussed in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—spirits and humans, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, intimate and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, affairs of government, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order of graded distinctions. To us today, how can such concrete social relations as spirits and humans, ruler and minister, father and son, husband and wife be placed on the same level as such abstract relative positions as noble and base, intimate and distant, near and far, superior and inferior? In fact, within our traditional social structure, the most fundamental concept—the organizing principle in the network formed by interactions among people—is precisely a differential order, that is, *lun*. The “Dazhuan” chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: “Loving one’s kin as kin, honoring the honorable, respecting elders as elders, and distinguishing between men and women—these are things that cannot be changed together with the people.” The meaning is that the framework of this social structure cannot be altered; what changes are only the things done by making use of that framework.

③ What Confucius emphasized most was the word “extend” (*tui*)—the outward expansion of ripples on water. He first acknowledged the existence of a self, the self from which one extends oneself to others. With respect to this self, one must overcome it through ritual propriety: to overcome the self is to cultivate the person. Following the concentric circles of ethical relations, one can then extend outward. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “Those who are filial and fraternal yet fond of offending their superiors are few indeed; those who are not fond of offending their superiors yet fond of creating disorder have never existed.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. The *Doctrine of the Mean* treats the five relationships as “the universal Way under Heaven.” Because, in this kind of social structure, one extends circle by circle from the self to all under Heaven, Mencius said that he was merely “good at extension.”

④ Within this highly elastic network, there is at all times and in all places a “self” serving as the center. This is not individualism, but egocentrism. The individual is spoken of in relation to the group: the member in relation to the collective. Under individualism, on the one hand there is the idea of equality, meaning that the positions of all members within the same group are equal and that the individual may not infringe upon the rights of all; on the other hand there is the idea of constitutionalism, meaning that the group may not efface the individual and may control the individual only over that portion of rights which the individual is willing to surrender. These ideas must first presuppose the existence of the group. In our traditional Chinese thought, there is no such set of assumptions, because what we have is egocentrism: a doctrine in which all values take the “self” as their center.

⑤ Egocentrism is not limited to Yang Zhu, who would not pluck out a single hair even if it benefited all under Heaven; even Confucianism should be included within it. The difference between Yang Zhu and Confucius is that Yang Zhu neglected the relativity and elasticity of egocentrism. He was too rigid-minded, clinging obstinately to the self and refusing to let go. Confucius, by contrast, knew how to extend from the self to others; yet even if this extension reached to the four seas, the center still remained the self. The Master said: “Governing by virtue is like the North Star: it dwells in its place, and the multitude of stars revolve around it.” This is an excellent metaphor for the differential mode of association: the self is always the center, like the Pole Star that does not shift through the seasons, while all other people revolve around it. Confucius was not like Jesus. Jesus had a group that transcended the individual—he had his Kingdom of Heaven—and therefore he could sacrifice himself in order to fulfill that Kingdom. Confucius, however, was not so.

⑥ Zigong said: “If there were someone who broadly conferred benefits on the people and was able to aid the multitude, what would you say of him? Could he be called humane?” The Master said: “Why stop at humaneness? He would surely have to be a sage! Even Yao and Shun would have found this difficult. As for the humane person: wishing to establish himself, he establishes others; wishing to achieve fulfillment himself, he enables others to achieve fulfillment. To be able to take what is near at hand as an analogy—this may be called the method of humaneness.”

⑦ In Confucius’s moral system, he absolutely refused to depart from the center of the differential mode of association: “The gentleman seeks it in himself; the petty man seeks it in others.” For this reason, he could not, like Jesus, love all under Heaven universally, even loving his enemies and praying to God to forgive those who killed him—such acts do not proceed from a self-centered starting point. What of Confucius? Someone asked: “What do you think of repaying resentment with virtue?” The Master said: “Then with what would one repay virtue? Repay resentment with uprightness, and repay virtue with virtue.” This is a hierarchy of differential levels, and Confucius never relinquished it. Confucius did not, like Yang Zhu, use the small self to respond to every situation; rather, he expanded or contracted the scope of morality according to need. He was unlike Jesus or the Chinese Mo Di, who, once they extended outward, could not draw it back.
