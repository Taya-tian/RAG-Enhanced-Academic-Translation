1. Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a load.

2. In China, this expression is extremely ambiguous.

3. This network is like a spider’s web: it has a center, and that center is oneself.

4. The concept of the “citizen” necessarily presupposes the concept of the “state.”

5. A group of people whose lives depend on one another cannot survive singly and in scattered isolation in the mountains and forests.

6. The idea of a constitution goes together with the Western idea of public office.

7. This social group is united for the birth and rearing of children.

8. On the father’s side, however, it may extend very far. A family of five generations living under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force that holds the group together.

10. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of living” to describe it.

11. “Those at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “the family gate” may refer to a large number of uncles, nephews, and cousins; “one’s own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle in order to show intimacy. The range of “one’s own people” can expand or contract according to time and place. It can become so large as to be beyond counting—truly, all under Heaven may become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I call the “differential mode of association”: a “network formed by one strand after another of personal ties.” This pattern is different from the modern Western “organizational mode of association.”

13. But as I now see it, this is not quite appropriate. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage-family.”

14. In its structure, the lineage-family includes the family; the smallest lineage-family may be equivalent to the family. This is because the structure of kinship is based on the parent-child relationship, the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, the clan is a corporate organization; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relationships of kinship to organize social groups and to conduct various undertakings, thereby making the basic family clan-like in character.

17. The Chinese family is a corporate organization; its size is determined by the size of the undertaking it carries on.

18. Since our family is a continuing corporate group, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. It is vertical, not horizontal.

19. I use this metaphor in order to make more concrete the pattern of relationships between people in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system is organizational in pattern, but it is utterly incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward without limit, including people of the past, the present, and the future. The common saying “one cousinship reaches three thousand li” expresses precisely this idea; indeed, “three thousand li” merely indicates its great breadth.

22. In the kinship system, we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. To go a step further, there are no two people in the world whose recognized kin are completely identical. Two brothers, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is similar to our circles of kinship. In the Grand View Garden of the Chia family, there could live Lin Tai-yü, a cousin through a paternal aunt, and Hsüeh Pao-ch’ai, a cousin through a maternal aunt; later there were still more—Pao-ch’in, Hsiu-yen, and so on. Anyone who could be connected as kin could be included. But once power and influence changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrank back into a small cluster.

24. Taking the self as the center, like a stone thrown into water, the social relationships formed with others are not like the members of an organization standing together on the same plane. They are more like the ripples on water, spreading outward circle after circle: the farther they spread, the more distant and the weaker they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The ten *lun* mentioned in the “Chi-t’ung” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, close and distant kin, rank and reward, husband and wife, affairs of government, elder and younger, upper and lower—all refer to graded distinctions. “Not losing the proper *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, close and distant. *Lun* is an order of graded differences.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born.” “Few are those who are filial and respectful to their elders yet fond of offending their superiors; none are there who are not fond of offending their superiors yet are fond of making rebellion.” From the self to the family, from the family to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a single path. In the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the five relationships are treated as “the universal Way of all under Heaven.”

27. A group is a “reality” that transcends the individual; it is not a tangible thing. We cannot point to some concrete object with physical form and say, “This is the group.” It is a bundle of relationships between people, a force controlling the behavior of individuals, an object on which the lives of its constituent members depend, a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot exist apart from individuals.

28. The “minister” or agent is a basic concept in a society organized according to the organizational mode of association. The clergyman who carries out the will of God is a minister; the official who carries out the power of the group is also a minister. Both are agents, not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and the clergyman, between the state and the government, must not be confused.

29. The social sphere pushed outward from the self is made up of one personal tie after another; each strand is maintained by a moral element. The social sphere is extended outward from the self, and in the process of extension there are various routes. The most basic is kinship: parent and child, and siblings. The corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect.

30. The parent-child relationship is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. This social group is united for the birth and rearing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such groups will not be absent.
