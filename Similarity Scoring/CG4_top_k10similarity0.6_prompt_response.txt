① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” The core of these terms is “family”; an adjective such as “small” or “large” is added to indicate the formal differences between the Chinese and Western “family,” assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite proper. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, in terms of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western chia. The distinction between what we commonly call the large family and the small family is by no means one of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but rather one of structure. A household with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions for being a “large family”; a household of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles employed in the two cases are different.

③ In anthropology, the concept of the family has a clear definition: it is the procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relationship is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. The group is united for the bearing and rearing of children. In any society where individuals undertake the task of bearing and rearing children, such a group will not be lacking. But the procreative function, for each individual family, is short-term. Once the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to conduct their own business of bearing and rearing children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Groups such as schools and states are not temporary; though in fact they are not eternal, neither are they provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the beginning prepare for its own ending. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring that rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture, the family as a social group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to the group constituted by parents and children, then both at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up there is a period when it is merely the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife may indeed carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they manage are greatly restricted. Any affair requiring the cooperation of more people must be managed by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are also very few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the chia has no strict corporate boundaries. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the graded sequence of kinship. Those who make up what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line of expansion is restricted. The line along which the Chinese chia expands is unilineal: it includes only the paternal side. With few exceptions, the chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are both members of outside families. On the father’s side, however, expansion can go very far. A chia of five generations under one roof may include all paternal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to this unilineal principle of kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Our chia, in structure, is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a tsu, for what we call a tsu is made up of many chia; it is a social group of social groups. For this reason I propose here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity, or size. This is why I am unwilling to use “large family,” and instead use “small lineage.” The difference of a single word explains the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relationship, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in a social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit by itself; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for undertaking enterprises; when further expanded, it can become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our chia is precisely like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted a differential mode of association, using the ethical order of kinship to organize social groups and manage all kinds of enterprises, so that the basic chia has become clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions may all be undertaken through the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to manage so many enterprises, the structure of the chia cannot be limited to the small grouping of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity, and this basic social group can by no means be temporary like the Western family. The chia must be continuous: it must not split apart when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the chia becomes that of a lineage. The clan is by nature long-term, just like our chia. To call our kind of social group a “small lineage” also indicates this long-term quality, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese chia is an organization for undertaking enterprises, and the size of the chia is determined by the scale of the enterprise. If the enterprise is small, the cooperation of husband and wife is sufficient to cope with it, and the chia may be as small as a family. If the enterprise is large, exceeding what husband and wife alone can undertake, then brothers, uncles, and other agnates may all gather together in one large chia. This explains why, in our rural society, the size of the chia can vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: a unilineal, differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to undertake many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly manage the affairs of procreation; children are supporting figures in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are undertaken by other groups and do not fall within the province of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force that holds the group together. The development of sexual affection has made their family the center for obtaining comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the chia shows a marked difference in this respect. Since our chia is a continuous enterprise group, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although the secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are nevertheless compelled by the needs of enterprise to exclude ordinary sentiment. By “ordinary sentiment” I mean that which stands in contrast to discipline. No enterprise can be separated from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of an enterprise group.
