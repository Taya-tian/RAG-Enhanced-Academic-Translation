① Western society is somewhat like the way we bundle firewood in the fields: several stalks of straw are tied into a handful, several handfuls into a sheaf, several sheaves into a bundle, and several bundles into a load. Each individual piece of firewood, within the whole load, belongs to a definite bundle, sheaf, and handful. Each piece can also be identified with the other pieces in the same handful, the same sheaf, and the same bundle; the divisions are clear and do not become confused. In society, these units are groups. When I say that Western social organization resembles bundled firewood, I mean to point out that it is often composed of distinct groups formed by a number of people. A group has definite boundaries: who belongs to it and who stands outside it cannot be left ambiguous but must be clearly distinguished. Those within a group form a body; their relation to the group is the same. If there are subdivisions or ranks within the same group, these too are stipulated in advance. My analogy of bundled firewood is not entirely apt in one respect: a person may belong to several groups, whereas it is of course impossible for one piece of firewood to be present in several sheaves at once. This is where people differ from firewood. I use this metaphor in order to make more concrete a certain pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call it the **group pattern**.

② In the West, the family is a group with clearly defined boundaries. If a friend writes to say that he will “bring his family” to visit you, he knows perfectly well which people will be coming with him. In China, however, such a statement is extremely vague. In Britain and America, “family” includes the man himself, his wife, and their minor children. If he were coming only with his wife, he would not use the word “family.” In China, although the phrase “the whole household is honored by your presence” is commonly seen, few people can say exactly whom this “household” ought to include.

③ Since we have touched on our usage of words, the character **jia**—“family” or “home”—is perhaps the most elastic of all. “The one at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “family line” may refer to a large group of uncles, cousins, and nephews; “one of our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s circle and toward whom one wishes to express intimacy. The scope of “our own people” expands or contracts according to time and circumstance; at its largest it may be beyond counting—indeed, all under Heaven may become one family.

④ Why should the term for our most basic social unit be so vague and indeterminate? In my view, this indicates that our social structure itself differs from the Western pattern. Our pattern is not like bundles of firewood tied up clearly one by one; rather, it is like the ripples that spread outward in circles when a stone is thrown onto the surface of water. Every person stands at the center of the circles produced by his or her social influence. Whoever is reached by the ripples of these circles becomes connected. The circle that a person mobilizes at a given time and place is not necessarily the same.

⑤ The most important kinship relations in our society have precisely this character of concentric ripples formed by a thrown stone. Kinship relations are social relations arising from the facts of birth and marriage. The network formed through birth and marriage may be extended outward indefinitely to include an infinite number of people—past, present, and future. The saying “a relative through a cousin may be three thousand li away” expresses this idea; “three thousand li” merely indicates the vastness of the possible extension. This network is like a spider’s web, with a center: oneself. Each of us has such a web extending outward through kinship relations, yet no two webs cover exactly the same people. People in a society may use the same system to identify their kin; what is shared is only that system. The system is an abstract pattern, or a set of categorical concepts concerning relations. But when we use this system to identify concrete relatives, the people identified differ from person to person. In the kinship system, we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. Going a step further, there are no two people under Heaven whose recognized kin can be completely identical. Two brothers do, of course, have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children. Therefore, as a network of social relations linked by kinship, it is individualized. Each network has an “I” at its center, and the center of each network is different.

⑥ In our rural society, this is true not only of kinship relations but also of territorial relations. The modern **baojia** system is patterned after the group model, but it is fundamentally incompatible with the traditional structure. In the traditional structure, each household takes its own position as the center and draws a circle around itself; this circle is the “neighborhood.” When there is a wedding or other celebration, people are invited to drink wine; when a child is born, red eggs are sent; when there is a funeral, people come out to help with encoffining and to carry the coffin. It is an institution of mutual assistance in daily life. Yet it is not a fixed group but a range, and the size of this range depends on the strength or weakness of the center’s influence. The neighborhood of a powerful household may extend throughout the whole village, whereas that of a poor household may include only two or three adjacent families. This is much the same as our kinship circles. In the Jia family’s Grand View Garden, for example, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin through the paternal aunt’s line, and Xue Baochai, a cousin through the maternal aunt’s line; later still more people appeared—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. Anyone who could be connected as a relative could be accommodated. But once power changes, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle contracts into a small cluster. At the extreme, it may be like Su Qin returning home in poverty: “his wife did not regard him as a husband, and his sister-in-law did not regard him as her husband’s younger brother.” The differential mode of association in traditional Chinese structure possesses this capacity for expansion and contraction. In the countryside, the family may be very small; yet among wealthy landlords and officials, it may become as large as a small state. Chinese people are especially sensitive to the warmth and coldness of human relations precisely because this highly elastic social circle expands or contracts according to changes in the power of its center.

⑦ In Western societies, where grown children living at home must pay their parents for board and lodging, people acknowledge the boundaries of the group. Those within the group possess definite qualifications for membership. Once those qualifications are lost, one must leave the group. For them, this is not a matter of the warmth or coldness of human feeling, but a matter of rights. In Western society, what people contend for is rights; in ours, it is connections that people seek to cultivate, and personal sentiments that they seek to invoke.
