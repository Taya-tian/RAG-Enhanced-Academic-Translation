**Group Four: Chinese and Western Chia**  
**Chapter Six, “The Clan”**

① What I wish to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” The main element in these terms is “family”; an adjective such as “small” or “large” is then added to indicate the formal difference between Chinese and Western “families,” assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite proper. A more accurate term for the basic social group in Chinese rural society would be the “small clan.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, in terms of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia. The difference between what we commonly call the large-family and the small-family is by no means a difference in size, nor in the number of persons included in the group, but a difference in structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large-family,” while a household consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small-family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles on which the two are based are different.

③ The concept of the family has a clear definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relationship is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. This group is formed for the birth and rearing of children. In societies where the task of procreation and child-rearing is borne by individuals, such groups cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, with respect to each individual family, is short-term. Once the children have grown up, they leave the nurture of their parents and go on to manage their own affairs of bearing and rearing children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite like ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not ad hoc, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must be prepared from the beginning for its own termination. The purpose of raising children is to bring the raising of children to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The Institution of Procreation*.

④ Yet in every culture, the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change in nature, and remains limited to the group composed of parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up there is a period in which it is only a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife may engage in economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the matters they can manage are greatly limited. Anything requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are also few, the chief one being the procreation and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may expand outward as needed along the graded sequence of kinship. The members who make up what I have called this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line of expansion is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line, that is, it includes only the paternal side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are both outsiders. On the father’s side, however, expansion may go very far: a Chia in which five generations live under one roof may include all paternal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed according to this unilineal kinship principle has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. In structure, our Chia is a clan. But it is not exactly the same as what we ordinarily call a clan or lineage, because what we ordinarily call a clan is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small clan.” The small clan and the large clan are the same in structural principle; what differs is number and size. This is why I do not wish to use “large-family,” but instead use “small clan.” The difference of a single word explains the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the clan includes the family, and the smallest clan may be equal to a family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relationship, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The clan is extended from the basis of the family. But the family included within the clan is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the clan includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for undertaking affairs; expanded further, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex functions—political, economic, religious, and so forth. Our Chia is just such a group. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted a differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to conduct various enterprises, so that the basic Chia has become clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the clan. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to conduct so many affairs, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small group of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, all political, economic, religious, and similar affairs require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous: it must not split apart because individuals grow up, nor end because individuals die. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a clan. A clan is by nature long-term, just like our Chia. To call this kind of social group of ours a small clan also indicates this quality of long-term continuity, in contrast to the temporary nature of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on affairs, and its size is determined by the scale of those affairs. If the affairs are small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the affairs are large and exceed what husband and wife can bear, brothers, uncles, and nephews may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relationships among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly conduct the affairs of procreation, while children play secondary roles in this group; once they have grown up, they leave it. For Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the family’s sphere. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes is the cohesive force. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia is markedly different in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous social group for carrying on affairs, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are, because of the needs of the enterprise, made to exclude ordinary feelings. By ordinary feelings I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No enterprise can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private sentiment. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. All these are characteristics of a social group organized for carrying on affairs.
