1. What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “Expanded Family.” In these terms the central word is “family”; an adjective such as “small” or “large” is then added to indicate a formal difference between Chinese and Western “families,” assumed to be alike in nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

2. My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the principle of structure, the difference between the Chinese and Western “family.” The difference between what we commonly call the large-family and the small family is certainly not a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but a matter of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large-family”; a household of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter, but structurally the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved are different.

3. The concept of “family” has a definite meaning in anthropology: it is a procreative group consisting of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation and rearing” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. This group is united for the bearing and rearing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is undertaken by individuals, such a group cannot be absent. But the function of procreation and rearing, for each individual family, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage the affairs of bearing and rearing their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states, though in fact not permanent, are not temporary groups, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation and rearing as its function, it must from the beginning prepare for its own end. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

4. Yet in every culture, the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains a group constituted by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is merely a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife may certainly carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Any affairs requiring the cooperation of a larger number of persons must be handled by other social groups.

5. In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. The members of this group may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who make up what I call the social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line of expansion is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line—that is, it includes only the father’s side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of outside families. On the father’s side, however, it may expand very far: a Chia of five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

6. A social group organized according to this unilineal kinship principle has a technical name in anthropology: the clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not quite the same as what we ordinarily call a lineage or clan, for what we call a lineage is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small lineage.” A small lineage and a large lineage are the same in structural principle; they differ only in number and size. This is why I do not wish to use the term “large-family,” but instead use “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

7. Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own. It is not a corporate group.

8. Differences in form also bring changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for common undertakings; expanded further, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes have complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely like this. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted a differential mode of association, using the moral norms of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like in character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small grouping of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous: it must not split apart when an individual grows up, nor end when an individual dies. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a lineage. The clan is by nature long-lasting, just as our Chia is. To call this kind of social group a “small lineage” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast with the temporary character of the family.

9. The Chinese Chia is an organization for common undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, and the cooperation of husband and wife is enough to handle it, the Chia may be so small as to be equivalent to a family. If the undertaking is large and exceeds what husband and wife can bear, then brothers, uncles, and other kin may all gather together in one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, differential mode of association.

10. When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly manage the affairs of procreation and child-rearing; children play a secondary role in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the province of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes becomes the force of cohesion. The development of this affection makes the family a center for obtaining comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. In our rural society, the nature of the Chia is markedly different in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for common undertakings, its main axes are between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; they are vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are, under the demands of common undertakings, stripped of ordinary sentiment. By “ordinary sentiment” I mean sentiment as opposed to discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feeling. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of an organization for common undertakings.
