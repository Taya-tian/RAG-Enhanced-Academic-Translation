**Group Four: The Chinese and Western Chia (Chapter Six, “The Lineage”)**

① What I should like to bring forward for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” In these terms the principal word is “family”; an adjective such as “large” or “expanded” is added to indicate a formal difference between Chinese and Western families, assumed to be of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite proper. A more accurate term for the basic social group of Chinese rural society would be the “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia in terms of structural principle. The difference between what we ordinarily call a large-family and a small-family is by no means one of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but one of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby qualify as a “large-family,” while a Chia consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small-family.” Numerically the former is larger than the latter, but structurally the latter is more complex than the former. The principles employed in the two are different.

③ The concept of family has a definite definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group consisting of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. This group is united for the birth and nurture of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is entrusted to individuals, such groups cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, so far as each individual family is concerned, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the nurture of their parents and go on to undertake the business of producing and rearing children of their own, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary; although in fact they are not permanent, neither are they provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the beginning be prepared for its own ending. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains confined to a group made up of parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is only a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife may indeed carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Matters requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate group; as I have explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who make up what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which it expands structurally is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line, including only the father’s side. Except in a few cases, the Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, the son-in-law and the married daughter are both people of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, expansion may extend very far: a Chia of five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to this unilineal kinship principle has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not exactly the same as what we ordinarily call a Tsu, because what we call a Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group of social groups. For this reason, I propose here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are the same in structural principle; what differs is quantity, or size. This is why I do not wish to use “large-family,” but instead “small lineage.” The change of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family. The smallest lineage may even be equal to a family, because the foundation of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended outward from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in the social circle. We cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can we say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the procreative function, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for undertaking affairs; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the moral order of kinship to organize social groups and conduct various undertakings, thus giving the basic Chia a clan character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that, in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary, as the Western family is. The Chia must be continuous; it must not split apart when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of the lineage. The clan is by nature long-lasting, as is our Chia. To call this social group of ours a small lineage is also to indicate this long-term character, in contrast to the temporariness of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for undertaking affairs, and the size of the Chia is determined by the scale of the undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large, exceeding what husband and wife alone can bear, then brothers and paternal uncles may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But however great the differences in size, the structural principle remains consistent: a unilineal, differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family group, husband and wife are the main axis. Husband and wife jointly undertake the procreative function; children are secondary figures in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. For them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the sentiment between the sexes is the cohesive force. The development of sexual affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑪ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for undertaking affairs, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are forced by the needs of the undertaking to exclude ordinary sentiments. By “ordinary sentiments” I mean those contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can be separated from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feelings. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of an undertaking-oriented social group.
