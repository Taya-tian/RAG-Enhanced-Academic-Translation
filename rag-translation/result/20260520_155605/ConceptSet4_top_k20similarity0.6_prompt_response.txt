**Group Four: The Chinese and Western Chia  
Chapter Six, “The Lineage”**

① What I wish to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” In these terms, the main word is “family,” with the adjective “small” or “large” added to indicate a formal difference between the Chinese and Western “family,” which are assumed to be of the same nature. But I now feel that this is not quite proper. A more accurate term for the basic social group of Chinese rural society would be “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the standpoint of structural principle, the difference between the Chinese and Western forms of the “family.” The difference between what we commonly call the large-family and the small-family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of people included in the group, but of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby meet the conditions of a “large-family,” while a family consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small-family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles used in the two cases are different.

③ The concept of family has a definite definition in anthropology: it is the procreative group formed by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married couple. The group is joined together for the birth and upbringing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is undertaken by individuals, such a group will not be lacking. But the procreative function, for each individual family, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage their own affairs of procreation and child-rearing, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states are not temporary groups; although in fact they are not permanent either, they are not provisional, because their functions are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must prepare for its own ending from the very beginning. The purpose of raising children is precisely to bring child-rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Procreation*.

④ Yet in any culture, the family as a social group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. Cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to the group formed by parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up there will be a period in which it is merely the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do of course carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they manage are subject to great limitations. All matters requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be managed by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are also few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. The members of this group may, according to need, extend outward along the differential order of kinship. The members who constitute what I have called this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which the structure expands is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line: it includes only the father’s side. Apart from a few exceptions, the Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of another Chia. On the father’s side, however, the Chia may extend very far. A Chia of “five generations under one roof” may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. Yet it is not exactly the same as what we ordinarily call a tsu, for what we call a tsu is made up of many Chia; it is a social group of social groups. For this reason, I have proposed here the term “small lineage.” The small lineage and the large lineage are the same in structural principle; they differ in quantity, in size. This is why I do not wish to use the term “large-family,” but instead use “small lineage.” The difference of one word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ In structure, the lineage includes the family. The smallest lineage may even be equal to a family. This is because the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended out from the basis of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring within the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit by itself; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on affairs; when further enlarged, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes are endowed with complex functions—political, economic, religious, and so on. Our Chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted a differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on all kinds of affairs, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions may all be undertaken through the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on so many affairs, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small group of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group cannot be temporary, as the Western family is. The Chia must be continuous; it must not split apart when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of the clan. The clan is by nature long-term, just like our Chia. To call this sort of social group a small lineage is also to indicate this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary nature of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on affairs, and its size is determined by the size of those affairs. If the affairs are small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the affairs are large and exceed what husband and wife can undertake, brothers, uncles, and other patrilineal relatives may all gather in one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society can vary so greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal differential order.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to undertake many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the principal axis. Husband and wife jointly carry on the affairs of procreation; children play a secondary role in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Among Westerners, political, economic, religious, and other functions are undertaken by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife thus become the principal axis, and the emotions between the sexes serve as the cohesive force. The development of sexual and emotional feeling makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for carrying on affairs, its principal axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the principal axis, is not temporary, both axes are made, by the needs of the enterprise, to exclude ordinary feelings. By ordinary feelings I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No enterprise can escape considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must speak of discipline; discipline excludes the tolerance of private sentiment. In the Chinese family there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children there is emphasis on responsibility and obedience. These are all characteristics of an enterprise-like social group.
