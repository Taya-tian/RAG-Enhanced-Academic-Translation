**Section Four: The Chia in China and the West  
Chapter Six: “The Lineage”**

① What I wish to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, a group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it the “Expanded Family.” In these terms, the main word is “family,” with an adjective such as “large” or “small” added to indicate a formal difference between the Chinese and Western “family,” which is assumed to be the same in nature. But now it seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group in Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the standpoint of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia. The difference between what we ordinarily call the large-family and the small-family is certainly not a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but a matter of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby become a “large-family,” while a family of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small-family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved are different.

③ In anthropology the concept of family has a definite meaning: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the spouses. The group is united for the birth and nurture of children. In any society where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function of each individual family is short-term. When the children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage the procreation and nurture of their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite like ordinary social groups. Schools and states, though in fact not permanent, are not temporary groups, for the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must, from the very beginning, prepare for its own conclusion. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown. If the family does not change its nature, and remains limited to a group composed of parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation, and again after the children have grown, there is a period in which it is only a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation; but the affairs they can undertake are greatly limited. Any undertaking requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be managed by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate group. As I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are few, chiefly the procreation and nurture of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members can, according to need, expand outward along the differential sequence of kinship. The persons who constitute what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Structurally, however, the line of expansion is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line; that is, it includes only the paternal side. Apart from a few exceptions, the Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of another Chia. On the father’s side, however, expansion can go very far. A Chia of five generations under one roof may include all paternal relatives within those five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to this unilineal kinship principle has a special name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. Yet it is not quite the same as what we ordinarily call a lineage, because what we call a lineage is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason, I have proposed here the term “small lineage.” A small lineage and a large lineage are the same in structural principle; their difference lies in number, in size. This is why I do not wish to use “large-family,” but instead use “small lineage.” The difference of one word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the foundation of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended outward from the basis of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring in the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit by itself. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the lineage includes the procreative function, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, a clan is an organization for carrying on enterprises; when further expanded, it can become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this sort. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted a differential mode of association, using the moral relations of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various enterprises, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like in nature. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage. On the other hand, we may also say that in order to carry on so many enterprises, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children; it must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group certainly cannot be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous: it must not divide simply because individuals grow up, nor come to an end because individuals die. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a lineage. The clan is by nature long-term, just like our Chia. To call this kind of social group a small lineage also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on enterprises, and the size of the Chia is determined by the size of the enterprise. If the enterprise is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the enterprise is large, exceeding what husband and wife alone can manage, then brothers, paternal uncles, and others may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly carry on the work of procreation and nurture; children are secondary actors in this group, and when they grow up they leave it. Political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the sexes is the force of cohesion. The development of sexual affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the phrase “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous enterprise group, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are nevertheless compelled by the needs of enterprise to exclude ordinary feelings. By “ordinary feelings” I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No enterprise can escape considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must emphasize discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private emotions. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are stressed. These are all characteristics of an enterprise group.
