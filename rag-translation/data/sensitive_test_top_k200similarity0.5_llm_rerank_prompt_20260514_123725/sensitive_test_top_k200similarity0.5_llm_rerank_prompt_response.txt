9. Husband and wife become the main axis, and the affection between the two sexes is the force of cohesion.

10. In my book *American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

11. “The one at home” may refer to one’s wife alone; “my family door” may refer to a large number of uncles, cousins, and nephews; “our own people” may include anyone whom one wishes to draw into one’s own circle and toward whom one wishes to show intimacy. The scope of “one’s own people” expands and contracts according to time and place; it may become so large as to be beyond counting—indeed, all under Heaven may become one family.

12. The basic structure of Chinese rural society is what I have called a “differential mode of association,” a “network made up of strands of personal relations.” This pattern is different from the modern Western “organizational mode of association.”

13. But as I now see it, this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

14. In structure, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be identical with the family. For the foundation of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child.

15. In anthropological terms, a clan is a corporate organization for carrying on common undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe.

16. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society has adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical order of kinship to organize social groups and conduct various undertakings, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like in character.

17. The Chinese Chia is a corporate organization for common undertakings, and the size of the Chia is determined by the scale of those undertakings.

18. Since our Chia is a continuing corporate community, its main axis lies between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal.

19. In using this metaphor, I am trying to make more concrete a pattern of interpersonal relations in social life. We may call it the organizational mode of association.

20. The modern pao-chia system is of the organizational mode of association, but it is incompatible with the traditional structure.

21. The network formed through procreation and marriage can be extended outward without limit to include an infinite number of people—past, present, and future. The saying “one cousin relationship extends three thousand li” expresses just this meaning; in fact, “three thousand li” merely indicates the breadth of its possible extension.

22. In the kinship system, we all have parents, but my parents are not your parents. Going a step further, there are no two persons under Heaven whose recognized relatives can be completely identical. Two brothers may of course have the same parents, but each has his own wife and children.

23. This is generally true of our kinship circle. In the Grand View Garden of the Jia family, for example, there could live Lin Daiyu, a cousin through the father’s sister, and Xue Baochai, a cousin through the mother’s sister; later there were still more—Baoqin, Xiuyan, and others. Whoever could be connected as a relative could be accommodated. But when power and fortune changed, “when the tree falls, the monkeys scatter,” and the circle shrank into a small group.

24. Taking the “self” as the centre, like a stone thrown into water, the social relations formed with others are not like the members of an organization standing on the same plane. Rather, they are like ripples on the water, spreading outward circle by circle; the farther they extend, the more distant and thinner they become.

25. *Lun* emphasizes distinctions. The ten *lun* mentioned in the “Jitong” chapter of the *Book of Rites*—ghosts and spirits, ruler and minister, father and son, noble and base, intimate and distant, rank and reward, husband and wife, political affairs, elder and younger, superior and inferior—all refer to graded differences. “Not losing one’s *lun*” means distinguishing father and son, near and far, intimate and distant. *Lun* is an order of differentiated grades.

26. “When the root is established, the Way is born”; “A man who is filial and fraternal yet fond of offending his superiors is rare indeed; one who is not fond of offending his superiors yet is fond of making disorder has never existed.” From oneself to the Chia, from the Chia to the state, and from the state to all under Heaven—this is a continuous path. In the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the Five Relationships are treated as “the universal Way under Heaven.”

27. A group is a “reality” transcending the individual, but it is not a tangible thing. We cannot point to some concrete object with physical form and say: this is the group. It is a bundle of relations between persons; it is a force controlling the conduct of each individual; it is an object on which the life of its component members depends; it is a common will that precedes any individual and yet cannot be separated from individuals.

28. The “minister,” or agent, is a basic concept in a society organized according to the organizational mode of association. The pastor who carries out the will of God is a minister; the official who carries out the power of the group is also a minister. Both are “agents,” not God or the group itself. The distinction between God and pastor, between state and government, must not be confused.

29. The social sphere constructed by extension outward from the self consists of strands of personal connection, each maintained by a moral element. The social sphere is pushed outward from the “self,” and in this process of extension there are various routes. The most basic is kinship: parent-child and sibling relations; the corresponding moral elements are filial piety and fraternal respect.

30. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; children are limited to those born of the spouses. The association of this community is for the birth and nurture of children. In societies where individuals undertake the task of bearing and rearing children, such a community cannot be absent.
