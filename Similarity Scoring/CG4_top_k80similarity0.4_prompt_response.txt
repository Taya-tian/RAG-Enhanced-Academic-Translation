① What I wish to raise for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large-family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “Expanded Family.” In these terms, the substantive element is “family”; a modifying adjective, “large” or “small,” is then added to indicate the formal difference between the Chinese and Western “family,” which are assumed to be alike in nature. But it now seems to me that this is not appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage-family.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia from the standpoint of structural principles. The difference between what we commonly call the large-family and the small-family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but a matter of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large-family”; a Chia consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot therefore be called a “small-family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles employed in the two cases are different.

③ The concept of the family has a definite definition in anthropology: it is a procreative group composed of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married pair. This group is united for the birth and nurture of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is undertaken by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, for each individual family, is short-term. When the children grow up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage the affairs of producing and rearing their own children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary groups; although in fact they are not permanent, they are not provisional, because the functions they possess are long-term. Since the family takes procreation as its function, it must from the beginning prepare for its own end. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring the rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in any culture, the family as a social group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when the children have grown up. If the family does not change its nature and remains limited to a group composed of parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is only a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation; but the affairs they manage are greatly limited. Any affair requiring the cooperation of a larger number of persons must be managed by other social groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group; as I have already explained above, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are few, chiefly the bearing and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential sequence of kinship relations. The members constituting what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line of structural expansion is restricted. The expansion of the Chinese Chia follows a unilineal line: it includes only the patrilineal side. Apart from a few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law at the same time. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters are people of another Chia. On the father’s side, however, expansion may extend very far; a Chia of five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to the principle of unilineal kinship has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not exactly the same as what we ordinarily call a Tsu, for what we call a Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group of social groups. For this reason, I have here proposed the term “small lineage-family.” The small lineage-family and the large lineage-family are the same in structural principle; their difference lies in number, in size. This is why I am unwilling to use “large-family,” and instead use “small lineage-family.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the lineage-family includes the family; the smallest lineage-family may even be equal to a family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage-family is extended out from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage-family is only one ring in the social circle. It cannot be said not to exist, but neither can it be said to form an independent unit by itself; it is not a corporate body.

⑧ Differences in form also give rise to changes in nature. Although the lineage-family includes the procreative function, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on affairs; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this sort. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relations of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various enterprises, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the lineage-family; on the other hand, we may also say that, in order to carry on these many affairs, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small combination of parents and children, but must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and other affairs all require long-term continuity; this basic social group can by no means be temporary, like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous: it must not split apart when an individual grows up, nor come to an end when an individual dies. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of a Tsu. The clan is by nature long-term, just as our Chia is. Calling this kind of social group a small lineage-family also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on affairs, and the size of the Chia is determined by the scale of those affairs. If the affairs are small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient to deal with them, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the affairs are large and exceed what husband and wife alone can bear, then brothers, uncles, and other patrilineal relatives may all gather into one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society can vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: a unilineal, differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife form the main axis. Husband and wife jointly manage the affairs of reproduction; children play a secondary role in the group and leave it when they grow up. For them, political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Since husband and wife form the main axis, affection between the sexes becomes the cohesive force. The development of sexual affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑪ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous group for carrying on affairs, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, ordinary feelings are excluded from both axes by the requirements of the group’s affairs. By “ordinary feelings” I mean feelings as contrasted with discipline. No enterprise can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feelings. In the Chinese family there is family law; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; women are held to the standards of the “Three Obediences and Four Virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. These are all characteristics of a social group organized for carrying on affairs.
