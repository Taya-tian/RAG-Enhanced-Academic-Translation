① What I wish to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “expanded family.” The principal term in these expressions is “family,” with an adjective such as “large” or “small” added to indicate the formal difference between the Chinese and Western types of what is assumed to be the same kind of “family.” But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small lineage.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia from the standpoint of structural principle. The difference between what we commonly call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large family,” while a family of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles involved in the two cases are different.

③ The concept of the family has a definite meaning in anthropology: it is a procreative group consisting of parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreation and rearing” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, including both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married pair. The group is formed for the birth and upbringing of children. In societies where the task of bearing and rearing children is borne by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, so far as each particular family is concerned, is short-term. When the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to conduct their own affairs of begetting and rearing children, generation after generation. The family as a group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite the same as ordinary social groups. Schools and states are not temporary groups; though in fact they are not permanent either, they are nevertheless not provisional, because the functions they perform are long-term. Since the family has procreation as its function, it must from the beginning be prepared to end. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to bring rearing to an end. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The System of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family group is always endowed with functions other than procreation. The cooperation between husband and wife does not end when their children have grown up. If the family does not change in character, and remains limited to a group composed of parents and children, then at the beginning of its formation and again after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is simply a union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation, but the affairs they can conduct are greatly limited. Anything requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people must be handled by other groups.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained above, it has strict group boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can undertake are few, chiefly the begetting and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society, the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may be extended outward as need requires along the differential order of kinship relations. The members who make up what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which it expands structurally is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands unilaterally—that is, it includes only the father’s side. Apart from a few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, the son-in-law and the married daughter are both members of an outside family. On the father’s side, however, the expansion may go very far: a Chia of “five generations under one roof” may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group organized according to the principle of unilateral kinship has a technical name in anthropology: the clan. In structure, our Chia is a clan. But it is not entirely the same as what we ordinarily call a Tsu, for what we call a Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a group of groups. For this reason I introduce here the term “small lineage.” A small lineage and a large lineage are the same in structural principle; they differ only in quantity, in size. This is why I prefer not to use “large family,” but instead “small lineage.” The difference of a single word indicates the structural character of this social group.

⑦ In structure, the lineage includes the family; the smallest lineage may even be equivalent to a family. This is because the basis of the kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The lineage is extended from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the lineage is only one ring within the social circle. One cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can one say that it forms an independent unit of its own. It is not a corporate group.

⑧ The difference in form also brings about a change in character. Although the lineage includes the function of procreation, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, the clan is an organization for carrying on affairs; when enlarged, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is precisely of this kind. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted a differential mode of association, used the ethical relations of kinship to combine social groups, and conducted all sorts of affairs through them, thus making the basic Chia clan-like. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be undertaken through the lineage; on the other hand, we may also say that, in order to conduct so many affairs, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small grouping of parents and children, but must be enlarged. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group cannot, like the Western family, be temporary. The Chia must be continuous: it must not split apart when an individual grows up, nor come to an end when an individual dies. Thus the character of the Chia becomes that of the clan. The clan is by nature long-lasting, as is our Chia. To call this kind of group a small lineage also conveys this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary character of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on affairs, and its size is determined by the scale of those affairs. If the affairs are small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the affairs are large and exceed what husband and wife can bear, then brothers and paternal uncles may all be gathered into one large Chia. This explains why, in our rural society, the size of the Chia may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is the unilateral, patrilineal, differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to undertake many other functions, the content of the relations among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife are the main axis. Husband and wife jointly conduct the affairs of procreation, while the children play supporting roles in the group and leave it once they have grown up. Political, economic, religious, and other functions are undertaken by other groups and do not fall within the family’s sphere. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes becomes the cohesive force. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes the family the center from which one obtains comfort in life. In my book *The American Character*, I used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑪ In our rural society, the character of the Chia is markedly different in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous organization for carrying on affairs, its main axes are those between father and son and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. They are vertical, not horizontal. The husband-wife relation becomes a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are nevertheless, because of the needs of the common undertaking, made to exclude ordinary affection. By “ordinary affection” I mean something contrasted with discipline. No undertaking can be divorced from considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private feelings. In the Chinese Chia there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be mutual respect; for women there are the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children there is an emphasis on responsibility and obedience. These are all characteristics of an undertaking group.
