**Group Four: The Chinese and Western Chia (Chapter Six, “The Clan-Family”)**

① What I wish to bring up for discussion here is the basic social group in our rural society, the group commonly called the “large family.” In *Peasant Life in China* I called it an “Expanded Family.” In these terms, the main word is “family,” with an adjective such as “small” or “large” added to indicate formal differences between Chinese and Western “families,” which are assumed to be essentially of the same nature. But it now seems to me that this is not quite appropriate. It would be more accurate to call the basic social group of Chinese rural society a “small clan-family.”

② My reason for proposing this new term is that I wish to explain, from the standpoint of structural principles, the difference between the Chinese and Western Chia. The difference between what we ordinarily call the large family and the small family is by no means a matter of size, nor of the number of persons included in the group, but of structure. A family with more than ten children does not thereby constitute a “large family,” while a Chia consisting of only four persons—parents-in-law, son, and daughter-in-law—cannot be called a “small family.” Numerically, the former is larger than the latter; structurally, however, the latter is more complex than the former. The principles on which the two are based are different.

③ In anthropology the concept of the family has a definite meaning: it is a procreative group constituted by parents and children. “Parents and children” refers to its structure; “procreative” refers to its function. The parent-child relation is bilateral, referring to both father and mother; the children are limited to those born of the married couple. This group is united for the bearing and rearing of children. In societies where the task of reproduction is borne by individuals, such a group cannot be lacking. But the procreative function, so far as each individual family is concerned, is short-lived. Once the children have grown up, they leave the care of their parents and go on to manage their own affairs of bearing and rearing children, generation after generation. The family as a social group is therefore temporary. In this respect, the family is not quite like ordinary social groups. Schools and states, for example, are not temporary groups—although in fact they are not permanent either—because the functions they perform are long-term ones. Since the family takes reproduction as its function, it must from the beginning be prepared to come to an end. The purpose of rearing children is precisely to end the need for rearing them. I have discussed this point in detail in my book *The Institution of Reproduction*.

④ Yet in every culture the family group is always endowed with functions other than reproduction. The cooperation between husband and wife does not come to an end when the children grow up. If the family does not change its nature, and remains a group limited to parents and children, then both at the beginning of its formation and after the children have grown up, there is a period in which it is merely the union of husband and wife. Husband and wife do, of course, carry on economic, emotional, and sexual cooperation; but the affairs they can manage are greatly limited. Any affair requiring the cooperation of a larger number of people has to be managed by some other social group.

⑤ In the West, the family is a corporate social group. As I have already explained, it has strict corporate boundaries. For this reason, the affairs this group can manage are few, the principal one being the reproduction and rearing of children. But in Chinese rural society the Chia has no strict corporate boundary. Its members may, according to need, expand outward along the differential order of kinship. The persons who form what I call this social circle are not limited to parents and children. Yet the line along which it expands structurally is restricted. The Chinese Chia expands along a unilineal line, including only the father’s side. With few exceptions, a Chia cannot include both daughters-in-law and sons-in-law. Under the patrilineal principle, sons-in-law and married daughters belong to outside families. On the father’s side, however, expansion can extend very far: a Chia with five generations under one roof may include all patrilineal relatives within five generations.

⑥ A social group formed according to this unilineal kinship principle has a technical name in anthropology: it is called a clan. Structurally, our Chia is a clan. But it is not exactly the same as what we ordinarily call a Tsu, because the Tsu is composed of many Chia; it is a social group made up of social groups. For this reason I propose here the term “small clan-family.” The small clan-family and the large clan are the same in structural principle; the difference lies in quantity, in size. This is why I do not wish to use the term “large family,” but instead use “small clan-family.” The difference of one word indicates the structural nature of this social group.

⑦ Structurally, the clan-family includes the family; the smallest clan-family may even be equal to a family. This is because the basis of kinship structure is the parent-child relation, the triangle of father, mother, and child. The clan-family is extended outward from the foundation of the family. But the family included within the clan-family is only one ring within the social circle. We cannot say that it does not exist, but neither can we say that it forms an independent unit of its own; it is not a corporate group.

⑧ Differences in form also bring about changes in nature. Although the clan-family includes the function of reproduction, it is not limited to that function. In anthropological terms, a clan is an organization for carrying on undertakings; when further expanded, it may become a tribe. Clans and tribes possess complex political, economic, religious, and other functions. Our Chia is just such a group. My hypothesis is that Chinese rural society adopted the differential mode of association, using the ethical relationships of kinship to organize social groups and to carry on various undertakings, thereby making the basic Chia clan-like in character. On the one hand, we may say that in Chinese rural society, political, economic, religious, and other functions can all be borne by the clan-family. On the other hand, we may also say that, in order to carry on so many undertakings, the structure of the Chia cannot be limited to the small parent-child group, but must be expanded. Moreover, political, economic, religious, and similar affairs all require long-term continuity. This basic social group can by no means be temporary like the Western family. The Chia must be continuous; it must not split apart when individuals grow up, nor come to an end when individuals die. Thus the nature of the Chia becomes that of the clan. The clan is by nature long-lasting, just as our Chia is. To call this kind of social group a “small clan-family” also indicates this long-term character, in contrast to the temporary nature of the family.

⑨ The Chinese Chia is an organization for carrying on undertakings, and its size is determined by the scale of those undertakings. If the undertaking is small, the cooperation of husband and wife may be sufficient, and the Chia may be as small as a family. If the undertaking is large, exceeding what husband and wife alone can bear, then brothers, uncles, and cousins may all gather together in one large Chia. This explains why the size of the Chia in our rural society may vary greatly. But no matter how great the difference in size, the structural principle remains consistent: it is a unilineal, patrilineal, differential mode of association.

⑩ When a procreative group is made to bear many other functions, the content of the relationships among its members also changes. In the Western family as a corporate group, husband and wife are the main axis. Husband and wife jointly manage the affairs of reproduction; children play a secondary role in this group, and once grown, they leave it. Political, economic, religious, and other functions are borne by other groups and do not fall within the sphere of the family. Husband and wife become the main axis, and affection between the sexes is the force that binds them together. The development of sexual and emotional affection makes their family the center from which they obtain comfort in life. In my book *American Character*, I once used the term “fortress of life” to describe it.

⑾ In our rural society, the nature of the Chia differs markedly in this respect. Since our Chia is a continuous group for carrying on undertakings, its main axis lies between father and son, and between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law; it is vertical, not horizontal. Husband and wife become a secondary axis. Although this secondary axis, like the main axis, is not temporary, both axes are compelled by the needs of the undertaking to exclude ordinary feelings. By “ordinary feelings” I mean feelings as opposed to discipline. No undertaking can dispense with considerations of efficiency. To seek efficiency, one must stress discipline; discipline excludes the indulgence of private sentiment. In the Chinese Chia there are family rules; between husband and wife there must be respectful distance; women are subject to the standards of the “three obediences and four virtues”; between parents and children, responsibility and obedience are emphasized. All these are characteristics of a social group organized for carrying on undertakings.
